The Eleven-Year Load

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from forty-four years of a power utility’s bulk-supply billing sheets, preserved on microfilm in the Longyear Deposit, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Eleven-Year Load is the Historical Faculty’s name for an unidentified Late Combustion Age over-the-horizon radio installation — a machine that watched past the curve of the Earth by bouncing megawatts of radio energy off the ionised upper air — reconstructed entirely from the electricity bills it ran up. The sheets, their columns, and the physics of the sky those columns betray are record; the machine’s site, its purpose, its name, and everything it ever watched for are reconstruction, or are not recoverable at all.

The name MAREK — a checker’s rubber stamp, inked and re-inked across thirty-two years of monthly billing sheets — is the only personal name the surviving record attaches to what was, by the plain testimony of its own energy column, the largest single customer its electricity utility ever billed. The customer is otherwise a number: four metered accounts under holder 0071, drawing a combined average of about 2.6 megawatts continuously, day and night, for forty-four unbroken years of a local calendar whose epoch is lost. Nothing else about the installation survives — no plan, no photograph, no register entry, no line of its own text. What survives is consumption, itemised monthly under a tariff that split each account’s energy into day-rate and night-rate columns, and the reconstruction that follows is built from those two columns alone: from the way the load traded between its accounts at sunrise and sunset, swelled in winter when it should have shrunk, and breathed on a period of very close to eleven years. Each of those rhythms has exactly one physical owner. Together they identify the customer, to the satisfaction of nearly the whole discipline, as a member of a documented class: an over-the-horizon backscatter radar, a machine of continental reach that used the ionosphere as a mirror and therefore had to follow, hour by hour for four decades, the moods of a shell of plasma governed by the Sun. The same rhythms that identify it also date it — but only to a phase of the eleven-year solar cycle, not to a year, and the entry’s title records that defeat.

Customer 0071

The fragment is a single reel of silver-halide microfilm: the bulk-accounts division of an unnamed regional supply authority, photographing its ledger sheets for retention, in the ordinary way large firms of the period committed their paper to film. Each sheet carries the same printed head, quoted here as the reel renders it:

SUPPLY AUTHORITY — BULK ACCOUNTS DIVISION — CUSTOMER 0071 — RENDERED MONTHLY — MAXIMUM DEMAND (KVA) · ENERGY TAKEN (KWH): DAY RATE / NIGHT RATE · POWER FACTOR · SURCHARGES AND REBATES — CHECKED:

and, in the checked field, the stamp. The sheets run from local year 08 to local year 51 — five hundred and twenty-eight months, of which five hundred and twenty-seven are legible. The year is given in two digits of a fiscal calendar whose zero is nowhere stated. The reel reached the Longyear Deposit in a consignment of commercial records whose line in the surviving acquisition register carries a transposed consignment digit; it was therefore catalogued to a seed cooperative, racked with the cooperative’s film two shelves above its correct position, and was above the waterline when the chamber flooded and the coal seam’s pyrite turned the standing water acid. The utility’s correctly-shelved consignments stood in that water and are gelatin sludge. The reel was first read in full at +913 pT (2858 CE).

Four accounts make up the customer. Accounts I and II, described on their sheets only as HALL A and HALL B, carry almost the whole load between them — a combined 1.9 gigawatt-hours in an ordinary month. Account III, a steady few hundred kilowatts, has the profile of workshops and quarters. Account IV is small, remote, and strange: some tens of kilowatts at a supply point the sheet locates 140 kilometres from the others, held under the same customer number, in a tariff class the utility reserved for loads requiring an electrically quiet feed.

The dark-hours column

The decipherment begins with a symmetry. Halls A and B never rest and never run together at full draw: their energy trades between them in strict anti-phase, and the trade lives in the tariff columns. Hall B’s consumption sits almost wholly in the night-rate column; Hall A’s in the day-rate column. Month by month, Hall B’s total tracks the hours of darkness — longer winter nights, more night-rate energy — and the annual curve of that dependence is not a vague seasonality but a precise geometric object. The duration of darkness as a function of date is set by latitude alone, and fitting the ledger’s dark-hours curve recovers a controlling latitude near 46° north, to within about two degrees. The fit’s honest limit is stated in every treatment since the first: what the load followed was the day-night line over the patch of sky it worked, which for a machine of this class lies more than a thousand kilometres from its meters along an unknown bearing, so the site itself is placed only loosely — a mid-latitude installation in the northern hemisphere, and no more.

The second anomaly eliminates every mundane explanation the first one left standing. Hall A — the daytime account — consumes more per daylight hour in winter than in summer. A lighting load runs the other way; so does cooling; heating would load both halls alike. The daytime ionosphere is the one physical system at 46° north that is stronger in winter: in summer, heated upwelling carries molecular nitrogen and oxygen into the F2 layer, and molecular ions recombine with free electrons far faster than atomic oxygen ions do, so the summer layer, for all its sunlight, holds a thinner electron population than the winter one. The period’s own geophysicists called this the winter anomaly, measured it for a century and a half, and never fully closed its books. Customer 0071’s day-rate column reproduces it, year over year, in kilowatt-hours.

The only mirror at that size

The physics that turns two billing columns into an identification is the period’s own, and it is exact. A radio wave entering an ionised layer is turned back where its frequency falls below the local plasma frequency — approximately nine hertz times the square root of the electron count per cubic metre — and the F2 layer’s peak plasma frequency at mid-latitudes ran from roughly two megahertz on a solar-minimum night to above ten at a solar-maximum noon. Sent obliquely, a wave refracts back to ground at up to about three times that vertical limit, returning to the surface between roughly one and three thousand kilometres out. A transmitter that wished to reach past the horizon therefore had to work between about 5 and 28 megahertz, and had to move within that band constantly: down at night as the layer thinned, up by day — pushed from below, too, by the dense daytime D region, whose absorption falls off roughly as the inverse square of frequency and taxes the low bands whenever the Sun is up. No antenna spans that whole range efficiently. The documented practice of the class was to split the band across separate curtain arrays, each fed from its own hall — and the ledger’s two halls, trading load at the terminator, are two band-segments following the ionosphere across the frequency plan. Nor was the following done by a hand on a dial. Installations of this class sounded the sky continuously and selected their own working frequency, minute by minute, by machine; the trade between Halls A and B is an automated system’s own choices, logged in the only place they were ever written down — the bill.

What the machine was for, the class supplies and the ledger cannot. A megawatt-class average radiated power — consistent with the halls’ wall-side draw once amplifier efficiency and cooling are allowed — is far beyond any broadcast or point-to-point service of the period, and a broadcaster follows its audience’s evenings, not the terminator. The deciding detail is Account IV: a backscatter radar cannot listen beside its own transmitter, and the documented installations of the class placed their receiving arrays one to two hundred kilometres away on deliberately quiet ground, listening for the Doppler-shifted fraction of their own returning echo, tens of decibels beneath the standing return of the sea and the ground. Account IV, 140 kilometres out on a protected-quality feed, drawing receiver power and nothing more, completes the pair. The machine watched a sector of the Earth some thousands of kilometres deep. The ledger does not record which sector, or for whom, or against what; the bearing of the curtains is not in the record, and no reconstruction of it is offered here. Whatever name the installation carried — and works of this size carried names — is unrecoverable. The utility knew it as 0071. The Faculty’s name for it is a description of its electricity.

Dating against the Sun

Across the reel’s forty-four years, the whole system breathes slowly: in some runs of years the high band carries weight deep into the night, in others the low band owns whole winter days. The period of that breathing, recovered from the column totals over four full swings, is 10.6 ± 0.5 years — the solar activity cycle, driving the F2 layer’s electron budget from above. This is the finding of Adeyemi’s phase-comb, the now-standard method of sliding a consumption series against the reconstructed solar-activity record until the phases lock, and it is also the finding’s undoing. The solar record of the Combustion Age is known to the Faculty largely through beryllium-10 in polar ice, an annually noisy proxy; the cleaner proxy, carbon-14 in tree rings, is spoiled across precisely this interval by the age’s own fossil exhaust — the same dead carbon that broke radiocarbon dating flattened the solar signal in the rings (Ashworth-Nakamura’s standing result). Against the beryllium series, the ledger’s four cycles lock acceptably at three distinct placements, one solar cycle apart: local year 08 falling at +83, +94, or +105 pT (2028, 2039, or 2050 CE). The two-digit year column cannot break the tie, and no other document mentions the customer at all. Under the first placement the sheets end in +127 pT (2072 CE); under the others, in +138 or +149 pT (2083 or 2094 CE). The machine is thus dated by the Sun to a phase, and by nothing to a year — a fact the literature has learned to state without embarrassment.

The last four sheets

The reel’s final local years change character only in their totals. Account III thins by steps across the last six years, to a fraction of its long mean; the halls hold their combined average almost to the end, the sounder still choosing frequencies, the terminator still trading the load. In the last four sheets the energy columns of Halls A and B fall by stages to nil; Account IV follows within two sheets; the maximum-demand column goes to nil; the standing charge is billed in full each month; each sheet is stamped. The final sheet is dated local year 51, month 12. There is no correspondence, no closure notice, and no further reel.

Provenance and ground

The entry rests on the one reel and on published physics. The tariff arithmetic, the dark-hours fit, and the solar-phase analysis are Adeyemi’s, following Okonkwo’s general method of reading a transaction record kept for an unrelated purpose as the unintended log of a machine’s behaviour; the propagation physics — plasma-frequency reflection, oblique-incidence range, D-region absorption, and the winter anomaly — is the Combustion Age’s own, independently attested in its surviving technical literature and not at issue. The identification of the customer’s class is accepted by nearly all workers; the three-way ambiguity of its absolute date is accepted by all of them. Site, bearing, operator, and object of surveillance are not reconstructed, because nothing in the record constrains them.
