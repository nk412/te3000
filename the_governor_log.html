The Governor Log

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from the protective event log of a small hydro-electric turbine governor, kept to count the machine’s load rejections and never meant as a record of anything else, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Governor Log is the Historical Faculty’s name for 12,652 timestamped frequency excursions in the memory of a recovery-era turbine governor, which together record forty-six summers in which an unnamed upland works took the nitrogen for its bread out of the air by electric arc, on the night-time surplus of a river of glacier melt. The log, the fused floor of the furnace, and the isotopes in the valley’s graves and lake mud are record; the works’ name, its people’s word for what they made, and the shape of its days are reconstruction, and thin.

Rukhsana Jumabek, cut into the granite of an intake weir on a glacier-fed side valley of the western Tian Shan beside the numerals 2557, is the only name the works preserves. The weir fed a run-of-river turbine of the Late Combustion Age, rated at 420 kilowatts and built in +93 pT (2038 CE), which a community of the recovery centuries kept in service; from +612 to +658 pT (2557–2603 CE) its digital governor logged, every night of the melt season, one under-frequency excursion at dusk and one over-frequency excursion at dawn, each caused by a single electrical load of about 262 kilowatts being thrown on and off. The load was an arc furnace. Air blown through an electric arc at some three thousand kelvin forms nitric oxide; cooled fast enough, the oxide survives; oxidised in cold air and washed into water it becomes nitric acid, and on limestone it becomes calcium nitrate, which is fertiliser. The method was the Early Combustion Age’s first industrial fixation of nitrogen, run on falling water in a northern valley from −40 pT (1905 CE) and abandoned within a generation as several times hungrier for power than the catalytic process that replaced it. In a valley with water, no catalyst, and no pressure steel, it was the right chemistry. The Faculty’s derivation from the log puts the works’ output near five tonnes of fixed nitrogen a season, grain for a population on the order of a thousand; the graves show the valley’s population rising and its endogamy loosening as people came up the river toward it; the works itself survives as a ring of fused brick and a pit of quartz pebbles, because everything it made was designed by its chemistry to dissolve.

The night load

A governor holds a turbine at its rated frequency by opening and closing the gate on the water. The unit in the valley was a single impulse wheel on an isolated supply, with no other machine to share a load step, so a load of six-tenths of its rating thrown on at once dragged the frequency down until the gate caught up, and the same load dropped at once drove it up until the deflector caught the jet. The age’s governors logged every such excursion to non-volatile memory, because the count of load rejections and over-speeds set the inspection interval of the runner, and this one went on doing so for five centuries after the last inspection. The Faculty reads the log as Okonkwo’s school reads any machine record: as a census of something it was not counting. Two consecutive lines from the first season, with the operators’ tag in the free field:

2557-06-03 21:14:08  UF  47.2 Hz  gate 0.41>0.87  step +262 kW  clr 00:00:07  tag N
2557-06-04 05:52:31  OF  54.1 Hz  gate 0.87>0.40  rej  -262 kW  clr 00:00:05  tag N

The tag is the single Latin letter N on 12,640 of the 12,652 arc entries; it is the age’s chemical symbol, and the works used no other word for what it did that the record has kept. The pairs — 6,326 of them — give the arc’s hours directly: struck at dusk when the settlement’s own lighting and pumping were falling away, dropped at dawn, a mean of 9.4 hours a night, and never in any season outside the months when the river ran high on the ice. Over forty-six seasons the pairs sum to some 59,500 hours at 262 kilowatts, a little under sixteen gigawatt-hours; at the age’s own figure for the arc route, sixty to seventy kilowatt-hours per kilogram of nitrogen fixed, that is between 220 and 260 tonnes of nitrogen, some five tonnes a season, or about four kilograms in every hour the arc burned. Applied at the rates the age’s agronomy recorded for cereals, five tonnes dresses on the order of fifty to a hundred hectares of grain. In 6,326 rejections the runner never reached its trip speed.

The race and the slow step

The yield of an arc furnace is not set at the arc. At three thousand kelvin the equilibrium between air and nitric oxide stands between four and five per cent oxide, and the reaction that makes it — an oxygen atom striking molecular nitrogen, with an activation energy of some 318 kilojoules per mole — is fast enough there to reach that figure in milliseconds. The same reaction runs backward as the gas cools, and the works’ product was the fraction of oxide that the gas carried down through the range where the reverse still ran, roughly 2,500 to 1,700 kelvin, before it could unmake itself. Below that the oxide is frozen in: its decomposition back to nitrogen and oxygen is favoured at every ordinary temperature and runs at none of them. The age’s arc plants recovered one to two per cent oxide at the furnace outlet from the four or five the arc had made; the difference was the cooling. In the valley the quench was the river. The gas left the furnace through a jacket fed from the same penstock that turned the wheel, at the temperature of ice melt, and the operators’ whole craft, so far as a log of frequency can show a craft, was in holding the arc steady on a machine that had never been meant to carry it.

The slow step came after, and it was the one step in the works that the valley’s cold helped. Nitric oxide takes up oxygen to become the brown dioxide in a reaction that is third-order — second in the oxide, first in oxygen — and has, almost alone among the reactions the age used at scale, a negative activation energy: it runs faster as the gas gets colder. At one to two per cent oxide in air the first half is gone in some tens of seconds; the last tenth wants minutes and the last hundredth half an hour, because a reaction second-order in its own reagent slows as it thins its reagent out. The works held the gas that long in a masonry chamber whose floor survives, and then ran it up a tower of granite packed with quartz pebbles against a fall of water, where three molecules of dioxide and one of water give two of nitric acid and return one of oxide to be oxidised over again, so that the gas went round the towers until it was spent. The acid, dilute, was run over limestone. Calcium nitrate is a salt that deliquesces at half-saturated air at ordinary temperatures, dissolves entirely in the first irrigation, and moves through soil with the water to the root and, in excess, past it. Nothing of it was ever going to be found.

What the valley kept

The furnace is a ring of fused firebrick 2.1 metres across, the imprint of a disc; the electrode copper was taken, as copper always was, and the iron of the magnet that spread the arc into that disc is a stain. The oxidation chamber is a floor. The tower is a pit of clean quartz pebbles that a survey without the log would have called a riverbed. The works’ whole product survives in two places, neither of them the works. Eleven kilometres downstream, in the laminated mud of a moraine-dammed lake, forty-six consecutive laminae carry a nitrogen-isotope ratio about a part per thousand lighter than the laminae above and below them — the marker by which the Stratigraphic Commission recognises the Combustion Age’s own layer in every lake on Earth, reproduced in miniature by one arc, forty-six laminae thick. Fixed nitrogen carries the isotopic ratio of the air it was taken from, which is the zero of the scale; the nitrogen a field gets from dung has been through an animal and is heavy by several parts per thousand; a cereal grown on the one reads about two parts per thousand and on the other about seven, and a person reads three to five above whatever they ate. The valley’s graves show it. Collagen from burials cut after +620 pT (2565 CE) reads about three parts per thousand lighter than burials cut before, while the sheep in the same middens, grazed on the high pasture the nitrate never reached, do not move. Read by the age’s own rule for reading manure in a bone, the valley’s fields had stopped being dunged; read against its own sheep, the valley was eating the arc. The rule is the age’s own and cannot be run on the age: in a Late Combustion skeleton the reactor’s zero is in every tissue, from the grain and from the meat of animals fed on it, and no unfertilised baseline is left inside the food web to read a manured field against. It works on the valley because the valley was alone.

The same graves carry the other record. Across the two generations after the first arc entries the runs of homozygosity in the valley’s genomes shorten, and the incoming segments date, by their lengths, to arrivals that come in order of distance up the river — the settlement at the valley mouth within a generation of +612, the basin towns a generation after that. No rule of marriage attested anywhere on the high interiors produces that order. Movement does: people came up the water toward the grain. The Faculty declines to weigh, against the arithmetic of the log, what the valley paid for it; the log records the machine and the machine recorded the nights.

End of record

The seasons shorten in the log’s last decade — 148 nights of arc in the first ten summers, 96 in the last — and the first strike of each year comes later, as the ice above the weir gave less melt in a shorter run; the glacier’s own moraines stand in a dated sequence below it, and the Faculty reads the two records together. The last arc entry is a dawn rejection on the twenty-ninth day of the eighth month of +658 pT (2603 CE), cleared in five seconds. The machine was started again the following spring, as it had been every spring since the melt first carried it, and at start-up the governor wrote the line the firmware had written at every start-up the works ever saw:

2604-04-11 06:02:40  BOOT  fw 4.2.1  evlog fmt v2 DEPRECATED: not read by fw>=5; export before upgrade

No excursion follows it. The upgrade was never installed.

Provenance and ground

The log was read from the governor’s non-volatile memory in the powerhouse by the Institute for Deep Record Studies’ survey of the valley in +976 pT (2921 CE); its firmware, its event format, and the maintenance practice that caused it to be kept are bit-exact record from one repository in consignment L-III of the Longyear Deposit. The furnace floor, the chamber floor, the tower pit, the weir, and the graves are physical record and were surveyed in the same season; the lake core was taken two years later. Adeyemi-Lund, T. derived the works’ output and acreage from the pairs (The Night Load, Historiography, 2963); the derivation is to order of magnitude, since the log gives hours and load and not a gram of product. Halvorsen’s taphonomy accounts for what is missing: copper by salvage, iron by rust, the product by its own deliquescence. The arc route’s yields, its energy cost, the kinetics of oxide formation, decay, and oxidation, the absorption stoichiometry, and the isotope offsets between fertiliser, dung, crop, and bone are as the age measured them; everything the valley did with them is inference from the log.
