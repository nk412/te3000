The Standing Almanac

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a single struck nickel plate and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Standing Almanac is the Historical Faculty’s name for a Late Combustion satellite positioning and timing service — a constellation of thirty spacecraft in medium orbit that broadcast the time and, through it, the place, to a continent increasingly on the move — reconstructed from one nickel dedication plate struck for a spare ground unit and preserved in an inland deposit. The plate, its inscribed parameters, and the physics of the orbit are record; that the service went on broadcasting a description of itself after the description had stopped being true is reconstruction, and it is not seriously contested.

The name struck first on the plate — above the frequencies, above the dedication in nine scripts — is MERIEL AZHAR, and nothing else about her survives: not her office, not her dates, not whether the system was named for her, built by her, or lost with her. The Standing Almanac is the Historical Faculty’s designation for the machine the plate describes, a Late Combustion Age satellite positioning and timing service of the kind the period built several of, recovered by function only because no operating name for it survives on any durable medium. Thirty spacecraft flew in six inclined planes at an altitude of roughly 20,200 kilometres. Each carried an atomic clock and did one thing: broadcast, continuously, the exact time and a precise description of its own orbit. A receiver on the ground that heard four of them at once could solve, from the differences in their arrival times, for its own position and for the time itself — a fix good, at the service’s best, to within a few metres and a few billionths of a second. It worked for the whole of its design life and then, unattended, for a great deal longer. Its makers set its clocks deliberately slow, on the ground, so that they would run true once in orbit; and they ran true. The service reached its full constellation around +86 pT (2031 CE); it carried, through the decades that followed, a growing share of a continent’s people who were leaving one place for another; the last correction any ground station ever sent it was struck onto the plate as a date; and the constellation, having no way to know this, went on holding formation and announcing everyone’s position with complete confidence long after there was a ground station left to hear.

Set slow, to run true

The engineering problem the service solved first is the one it is now remembered for, and it is a problem in relativity. A clock’s rate depends on where it sits in a gravitational field and on how fast it moves. A clock lifted to 20,200 kilometres, where the Earth’s gravity is much weaker than at the surface, runs fast — by general relativity, about 45 microseconds a day. The same clock, carried around its orbit at 3.87 kilometres a second, runs slow — by special relativity, about 7 microseconds a day. The two effects do not cancel. The net is a gain of roughly 38 microseconds a day, every day, for every satellite in the constellation.

Thirty-eight microseconds sounds like nothing. It is not nothing, because the whole method is a method of timing, and in a timing method every error of the clock becomes an error of distance at the speed of light. A nanosecond is about thirty centimetres. A microsecond is about three hundred metres. Left uncorrected, the relativistic gain alone would have walked every fix in the service area outward at something like ten kilometres a day, and a position solved from the raw broadcast would have been useless within two minutes of being switched on. The makers knew this before the first satellite flew. Their answer was not to correct it in the receiver but to build the correction into the hardware: they manufactured each satellite’s oscillator to beat, on the ground, at a frequency very slightly below its nominal figure — a design centre of 10.23 megahertz was cut, on the bench, to 10.22999999543 megahertz — so that once the satellite was in orbit, running fast by exactly the predicted amount, it would beat at the true rate. They set the clock slow so that the sky would set it right. The correction is a fixed number, applied once, in the machine shop, to a clock that would spend its life where no one could reach it. It held for the whole life of every unit. This is the part of the record that admits no irony: the physics was understood exactly, the number was computed exactly, and the machines did exactly what the number said they would.

What the ground segment corrected, from day to day, was the smaller and less glamorous drift — the ordinary aging of a real atomic clock, and the slow wander of a real orbit under the pull of the Moon and the Sun and the flattening of the Earth. Each satellite broadcast a short-term prediction of its own path, the ephemeris, good for a few hours, and a coarse description of the whole constellation, the almanac, good for months. A master station tracked the satellites from the ground, computed where they had actually gone, and uploaded fresh predictions before the old ones went stale. The satellites could not do this for themselves. A satellite knows the time superbly and knows nothing whatever about where it is except what the ground last told it. The entire accuracy of the service rested on a single filament: one control segment, uploading corrections to thirty clocks that were otherwise flying blind.

What the fix was for

A positioning service is built for ships and for freight and for the guidance of weapons, and this one was built for those too. But the record of its use, in the decades of the road, is overwhelmingly a record of people leaving. The service came to its full strength in exactly the span in which the continent under it began to move — poleward, inland, upland, away from the coasts that were going under and the interiors that were drying out — and a receiver that could tell a person, on an unmarked track at night, exactly where they stood and exactly what time it was became one of the few instruments of that movement that actually worked. The scholarship reads the service, following Okonkwo, less as a navigation system than as an unintended census of the road: the pattern of where its signals were demanded, reconstructed from the intercepted ground traffic that requested and relayed them, traces the displacement more faithfully than any registry that tried to record it on purpose. The people who moved kept no archives. The thing that guided them kept the time.

It kept more than the time for them. Precise, common time is the hidden requirement of almost everything the Late Combustion built at scale, and the constellation had become, by the service’s maturity, the cheapest source of it anywhere in the service area. The phase reference that held the continental alternating-current grid together drew on it. The synchronisation of the long-haul data network drew on it. The other timing services of the age — the older ground broadcasts, the kind reconstructed as the Long-Wave Reveille — were narrower and dimmer beside it, and one by one the systems that had leaned on them leaned on the satellites instead. Ibarra’s doctrine of single-filament infrastructure — that the age ran its whole planet through dangerously few points of failure — has few cleaner illustrations. A continent’s position, and a great deal of its time, and through the time a great deal of its grid and its network, hung on thirty clocks in six planes and on one ground station’s daily habit of telling them where they were.

Free-running

The last correction the constellation ever received is a date, and the date is struck on the plate: the final line of the dedication face records the epoch of the last uploaded almanac as +138 pT (2083 CE). What happened at the ground station to make it the last, the plate does not say and nothing else recovers. It is one date among the countless the road produced. After it, the service did not stop. It could not stop. Nothing aboard any satellite was built to notice that the uploads had ended, because noticing was the ground’s job, and the ground was the thing that had gone. The clocks, tuned slow and running true, kept the time to specification. The onboard processors, finding no fresh ephemeris, did what they were built to do in that case — extrapolated forward from the last one they had — and went on assembling the navigation message and broadcasting it, every thirty seconds, on the same frequencies, with the same health flags set to healthy, to a service area from which the receivers were, year by year, being switched off and left behind.

The broadcast was, by then, slowly wrong, and getting wronger in a way no one aboard could correct. The extrapolated orbit and the aging clock drifted apart from the truth at a rate the ground would ordinarily have swept up every few hours; with the ground gone, the error simply accumulated. A clock wrong by a microsecond reports a range wrong by three hundred metres, and the constant that ties the one to the other is 299,792,458 metres a second, and no station on the ground and no cleverness aboard the satellite could bend it. The almanac — the constellation’s broadcast description of itself — went on being transmitted, faithfully, frame after frame, while the constellation it described drifted quietly out from under it. The system was, in the exact sense the scholarship means by the phrase, one of the clearest cases of the systems that outran the people: an automation executing a correct procedure on a world that had moved, keeping its record perfectly and keeping it about a thing that was no longer so. It is a near sibling to The Waiting List, the cryobank recommender that went on issuing correct pairings for a herd that had ceased to exist, and to the failure at the heart of The Wide Synchrony; the difference is only that this record was broadcast to a whole continent instead of written to a disk.

The end, when it came, came the way orbital ends come, which is slowly and by parts. The station-keeping propellant — the small store of thrust each satellite carried to nudge itself back into its slot as the Moon and the Sun tugged the orbit — was finite, and could not be replenished, and this is the hard edge under everything else: no upload, no ingenuity, no reallocation of any resource aboard could make more of it than had been loaded before launch. As each satellite spent the last of it, over the years around +150 pT (2095 CE), it began to wander out of its assigned place, still broadcasting, its slow drift now compounded by a slow displacement. The solar panels aged and the batteries lost their hold; the transmitters browned out one at a time and fell silent, the last of them some time in the third century pT. There was no failure and no alarm. There was a constellation that kept its formation until it could not, and kept the time until it could not, and told anyone still listening exactly where they were, to a precision that meant less and less, for as long as the sunlight held.

The museum that still talks

Everything the service did after +138 pT it did in orbits from which nothing falls. Medium orbit, unlike the low orbits that scrub themselves clean against the top of the atmosphere within years, has effectively no drag; a body left at 20,200 kilometres stays there for a span better measured in millions of years than in centuries. The thirty satellites did not come down and are not coming down. They belong to the same class of survival as the geostationary graveyard — the ring of dry, cold, intact dead satellites the scholarship calls the museum nobody visits — except that this museum, for a while, went on talking. The Selene Historical Society, which keeps the orbital register from Tycho, tracks what remains of the constellation the way it tracks the rest: by the same gravitational equations the makers used to place them, run forward a thousand years. The satellites are exactly where the physics says they should be. They are simply unresponsive.

Because nothing physical fell to Earth, the whole terrestrial anchor of the service is the plate — a spare or qualification unit’s dedication face, struck on nickel against exactly the media rot that erased everything else, and carried inland, poleward and upland, along the same road the service had guided, into a deposit where it kept. The plate’s later reading has settled. A minority holds, in the manner of the revisionist school, that the constellation’s persistence proves the continuity case in miniature — that a service still broadcasting is not a service that collapsed, and that the receivers went dark because their users had arrived somewhere and no longer needed a fix, not because they had died. The wider reading treats the broadcast-to-no-one as the plainest possible instance of the age’s signature: a competence that worked exactly as designed, understood down to the microsecond, running on after the understanding had nowhere left to land. The plate does not adjudicate. It carries a name, a table of frequencies, an epoch, and, beneath the parameters, in nine scripts, a single struck line that the Faculty renders:

SO THAT NONE WHO MOVE NEED BE LOST.

The line was cut to be read from the ground. It has spent the last eight centuries facing a rock in the dark, above a sky in which twenty-eight of the thirty are still in their orbital planes.

Provenance and ground

The reconstruction rests on a single artefact: a struck nickel identification-and-dedication plate, roughly 180 by 240 millimetres, of the kind bolted to a flight-qualification or spare satellite bus and retained on the ground at an integration facility. It was recovered inland and upland of the Late Combustion coasts, in a deposit consistent with the poleward displacement, and it survived by the same logic as the nickel disks of the deliberate archives — a durable, corrosion-resistant metal carrying human-legible engraving, indifferent to the media rot of the Attrition. The plate gives the constellation’s orbital altitude, plane count, and clock type; the broadcast frequencies; the dedication name; and the epoch of the last uploaded almanac. The relativistic design correction, the orbital parameters, and the free-running behaviour after +138 pT are not on the plate; they are reconstructed from the physics, which is fixed, and corroborated by a small number of broadcast navigation frames preserved in intercepted ground traffic (see the Wasatch Corpus), which match the plate’s parameters and carry epochs later than the plate’s final date. The satellites themselves remain in orbit and are catalogued but have not been physically examined. Whether the service the plate describes is the same one whose late frames appear in intercept, or one of the period’s several near-identical services, cannot be established from the plate alone; the Faculty treats them as one under a single designation and marks the identification provisional.

Notes

  • Dates are post-Trinity (pT); CE = pT + 1945. The constellation is dated to full strength at +86 pT (2031 CE); its last uploaded almanac to +138 pT (2083 CE); the exhaustion of station-keeping propellant across the fleet to around +150 pT (2095 CE).
  • The relativistic figures — a general-relativistic gain of about 45 microseconds a day, a special-relativistic loss of about 7, a net of about 38 — are properties of the orbit, not of the service, and hold for any clock flown at that altitude and speed. The uncompensated drift of roughly ten kilometres a day, and the on-ground pre-tuning of the oscillator to a fraction below its nominal frequency, follow directly from them.
  • The dedication name MERIEL AZHAR is attested only on the plate and is not otherwise recoverable; per convention, personal names are reported where a durable, name-bearing medium carries one, without inference as to whom they belonged to.
