# The Lowmarsh Refuge

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a single sealed mechanical tally counter, intact and unaccompanied by any register, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Lowmarsh Refuge is the Historical Faculty's name for a Middle Combustion dual-use storm and civil-defence refuge, built beneath a harbour-quarter car deck, whose roof slab failed in its forty-second year while an automatic corrosion-protection system was doing exactly what it had been written to do.** The counter, the slab's tendon ghosts, and a stretch of intercepted controller telemetry are record; what the counter counted, why the slab came down, and what the facility was called are reconstruction, and the last is unrecoverable.

---

**A bronze tally counter of five wheels, bolted to a stair wall at a height of 1.1 metres, shows the figure 11,46 followed by a units wheel that has stopped between its 1 and its 2.** It was recovered in the Institute for Deep Record Studies' resurvey of the Lowmarsh basin in +1,020 pT (2965 CE), still fixed to a section of reinforced wall that had fallen with the slab above it and been sealed ever since in silt. The counter is intact; its casing is closed; its wheels, once the crust is imaged through, turn freely except the last. It is the only object from the structure that says anything in numerals, and it says them in a form that records one thing — a single stair, a single direction — while the question it is asked to answer is about the whole building. The refuge it belonged to was a prestressed concrete floor under a harbour-quarter car deck, rated by its own stencilled plate for 2,400 persons; in +62 pT (2007 CE) its roof slab, protected for thirteen years by a controller that held a voltage to a setpoint, fractured and came down over a period of days. The Faculty maintains three readings of the fracture and has not been able to retire any of them. The name the structure was known by is not recoverable: the surviving plate gives its function and its capacity and leaves the line where a name would be cut as bare concrete.

## The counter

The counter is a ratchet totaliser of the ordinary Middle Combustion pattern: a lever, actuated by a turnstile arm, advances the units wheel by one tenth of a revolution; at each ninth-to-zero transition a finger on that wheel advances the next by one. The mechanism has no reset. The Faculty's reading of its casting marks places it in the manufacturing run of a municipal supply contract, and the absence of a reset key is documented elsewhere as a deliberate civil-defence procurement requirement: a count that could be returned to zero was not a count.

The casing is a tin bronze of the kind sold as gunmetal, with a few per cent zinc, and the choice of alloy is why the object exists at all. Copper-tin alloys hold up in seawater and in chloride-bearing damp far better than ferrous metal, and far better than the high-zinc brasses, which lose their zinc selectively in such conditions and leave a porous, weak copper sponge in the shape of the original part. Neutron tomography is the Faculty's method of choice for the counter, because neutrons pass through bronze with little loss and are strongly scattered by hydrogen, so that the hydrated corrosion products that fill the casing are seen against the metal with high contrast. What the scans show is a surface crust, one to two millimetres thick, of paratacamite and atacamite — basic copper chlorides, the green products of chloride attack on bronze, familiar to the discipline as the pale powdery growth on buried Bronze Age metal, and in the field called *bronze disease* because, once chloride is in contact with cuprous chloride trapped in the corrosion layer, the reaction regenerates its own acid and spreads. The crust is thickest on the units wheel and its pawl. It has not moved the other four wheels. It has locked the last one with the pawl halfway across a tooth, so that the figure reads either 11,461 or 11,462 and the counter itself cannot say which.

The figure is small enough to have been reached many times over by an ordinary use. Over the counter's service, from the structure's commissioning in +20 pT (1965 CE) to the slab's failure, 11,461 admissions across forty-two years is an average of about 273 a year, or 0.75 a day. That is the whole of what the number says, and it is less than it appears, because the counter was fitted to one of three entrances. The other two, ramp doors from the car deck above, were not counted at all.

## The refuge

The structure was a dual-use building of a kind common in the harbour cities of the Middle Combustion Age, and the Faculty counts its parallels in the dozens: a car deck above, serving a quarter whose lanes were too narrow for parking, and below it a single hall, 2,400 square metres in plan, which the municipality held in reserve for two purposes. In ordinary years it was drilled as a civil-defence refuge, with an annual practice admission through the stair and a posted capacity at one square metre per head. In flood years it was opened, at the municipality's discretion, as a storm refuge for the low quarter around it; the Lowmarsh basin is, as the name given it by the Faculty records, a marsh reclaimed by embankment, and its streets stood below the highest recorded tide.

The roof of the hall was a flat slab of post-tensioned concrete, a method by which steel strands are threaded through ducts cast into the slab, drawn tight by jacks, and anchored, so that the finished slab is held in compression and can span a width that ordinary reinforced concrete could not. The strands were cold-drawn high-strength steel wire, with an ultimate tensile strength on the order of 1,770 megapascals, tensioned in service to something like 60 to 70 per cent of that. After tensioning the ducts were filled with cement grout, which does two things for the steel: it bonds it to the concrete, and it surrounds it with a highly alkaline paste, a pH of 12.5 or more, in which iron forms a thin, adherent, invisible oxide film that corrodes at a rate on the order of a thousandth of a micrometre a year. This is the *passive state*, and it is the whole defence of reinforced concrete. It is chemical, not physical. It fails in two ways. The cement paste can be carbonated by atmospheric carbon dioxide over a few decades, falling toward pH 9 and dissolving the film. Or chloride ion can reach the steel in sufficient concentration — a threshold, widely cited, of roughly 0.4 per cent by mass of cement, with a wide scatter — and break the film locally, so that the steel under the break becomes a small acidic anode in a large alkaline cathode and pits.

Chloride was in plentiful supply. The deck above was salted in winter. The quarter's tide gate leaked. The refuge, by the time of the retrofit, had been wetted at least eleven times in its life by recorded flooding from below. Pitting rates in chloride-contaminated concrete reach the order of 1 microampere per square centimetre of local corrosion current, which Faraday's law converts to a loss of steel thickness of 11.6 micrometres a year; on a wire 7 millimetres across, unevenly attacked and already stressed at two-thirds of its tensile limit, a few hundred micrometres of pit depth is a failure, and the rust that fills a pit occupies between two and six times the volume of the steel it replaced, which is how concrete cover is cracked off a structure from within.

## The protection

In +49 pT (1994 CE) the municipality had the slab retrofitted with *impressed-current cathodic protection*. A titanium mesh anode, coated with mixed metal oxide, was fixed to the soffit of the slab and overlaid with sprayed mortar; a rectifier in the stair lobby, rated at 50 volts and 20 amperes, drove direct current from the mesh through the pore water of the concrete to the steel, which was made the cathode of the circuit. The principle is the inverse of the pit. If the steel is polarised negative enough, the anodic dissolution of iron stops, and the metal is held in a state in which it cannot give up electrons faster than they are supplied. The period's working criterion was a shift of at least 100 millivolts in the steel's potential, measured against an embedded reference electrode immediately after switching the current off, so as to exclude the voltage drop through the concrete itself, and a hold, at the controller, on a fixed potential. The controller measured the steel's potential against an embedded manganese dioxide reference electrode in the north bay, compared it with a setpoint, and raised or lowered the current to close the difference.

That controller's logic was among the files recovered from the Code Deposit, in a public repository of firmware for industrial controllers, with a commit message in the form of a pre-filled template. It is a proportional-integral loop of thirty-one lines, an ordinary and correct piece of control engineering, with one reference electrode feeding one loop per zone, a ceiling on output at the rectifier's rating, and a rule that if the measured potential fell short of the setpoint the loop was to keep raising the current. Nothing in it is wrong. The scholar Ibarra, N. cites it as the paradigm case of single-filament control in a small compass: a structure of 2,400 square metres, whose protection depended on the reading of one electrode in one corner.

The plate beside the rectifier is the one surviving inscription of the retrofit. It is stencilled in two languages and reads, in the lettering of the period: **PROTECTED STRUCTURE. DO NOT SWITCH OFF. THE SYSTEM CONTROLS ITSELF.** The third sentence is the one the Faculty has not been able to read as anything but literal.

## The eleven days

The rectifier's controller reported its state by modem to a remote monitoring service, once an hour in routine operation and at the rate of every minute during an alarm. That traffic, in the course of its transit, was intercepted, and it survives in the Wasatch Corpus, from which the scholar Okonkwo, D. has read the timestamps as an unintended log of the structure's last fortnight. They show the following. On the tide of the third night of a storm surge in +62 pT (2007 CE) water reached the car deck from the quay side and drained to the hall below, which filled to a depth that the Faculty places at between 1.6 and 2.3 metres, through an overflow chute that was designed to prevent exactly that. The north bay, in which the reference electrode stood, was the high corner of the hall, and the water did not reach it for four days. Its concrete stayed dry, high-resistivity, and well supplied with oxygen; its electrode went on reporting a steel potential less negative than the setpoint, as steel in such conditions is expected to do. The rest of the soffit sat in seawater, whose resistivity is of the order of 0.2 ohm-metre against hundreds of ohm-metres for dry concrete, and current, which takes the path of least resistance, went there. The controller read the shortfall at its one electrode, raised the output, read the shortfall again, and raised it again, and by the ninth hour it was delivering the rectifier's full 20 amperes into the wet two-thirds of the slab, where its measured effect on the steel was to drive the potential more than half a volt past the setpoint it had been written to approach. **The controller held that output for eleven days, in correct accordance with a rule that had never been wrong, until the supply at the street failed and the rectifier lost power.**

The consequence is a matter of electrochemistry. In alkaline pore water at pH 13, hydrogen evolves from water on a steel surface at a potential of about −0.77 volt against the standard hydrogen electrode, which is near −1.1 volt against the copper–copper sulphate electrode on which the period's field criteria were reckoned. Below that the cathodic current, instead of suppressing the dissolution of iron, goes into making hydrogen at the surface of the wire. A fraction of the hydrogen enters the steel as atoms and diffuses to the zones of highest triaxial stress, and in a high-strength, cold-drawn, heavily loaded wire — one carrying 1,200 megapascals for forty years — this is *hydrogen embrittlement*: a loss of toughness so complete that the wire fails by cracking at stresses it had carried for decades, without necking, and after a delay of hours to weeks. The ceiling that the period's later practice placed on potential for prestressed steel exists for this reason, and is known to have been learned at cost. The charge passed in eleven days at 20 amperes was 19 megacoulombs. If every electron of it had been spent on hydrogen, that is 98 moles of gas, or about 200 grams, and no more than a minute fraction of that could have entered the steel. A very small amount of hydrogen is sufficient.

The slab began to part some days after the rectifier stopped. It came down in sections over a period the Faculty reads, from the silt stratigraphy over the fallen blocks, as roughly nine days.

## Three readings

**The rule reading.** The first school, associated with Ibarra, N., holds that the wires failed by hydrogen embrittlement during and after the eleven days. Its evidence is the geometry of the failure and the telemetry. The slab's tendon ducts are filled with grout, and the grout has preserved, in the form of iron oxide pseudomorphs — a mineral fully replacing a prior form while retaining its shape — a cast of each wire as it stood. Computed tomography of the blocks recovered shows the wires as long rust ghosts of constant diameter, 143 of them cut across, and the fractured ends are flat and square, without the cup-and-cone narrowing of a ductile tensile failure. This is the signature of brittle fracture. The same school notes that 61 of the 143 fractures lie within the wet two-thirds of the soffit, where the current was.

**The grout reading.** The second school, associated with the Faculty's taphonomist Halvorsen, E., holds that the wires were dying anyway. Post-tensioned ducts of the period were grouted in a way that left voids at the high points of draped tendons, where bleed water rises and the grout settles away from the steel; a wire in a void is exposed to whatever moisture and chloride reaches it through the duct, with no alkaline paste to protect it. Brittle flat fractures also occur from pitting, where a pit acts as a crack starter and chloride-assisted stress corrosion does the rest. Cathodic protection does not reach a void, because the void is a gap in the electrolyte, and so the school reads the protection as irrelevant to the wires at the voids and the fractures as the working out of a pre-existing flaw. Its evidence is that 38 of the 143 fractures sit at the crests of the drape, in ducts with preserved void geometry.

**The record reading.** The third school, associated with Marchetti, L., makes no claim about the steel. It holds that the telemetry on which the first school's timeline depends cannot be trusted to say what happened on the stair. The monitoring service generated its hourly reports in sentence form from a library of phrases, and its human operators' notes, which survive in the same stream, were written from the same drop-down library: *Output at maximum. Reference reading outside range. Operator informed. Acknowledged.* Of the 211 entries in the eleven days, the Faculty's classifiers separate 84 as certainly machine-composed by their timing, and cannot classify the other 127 as human or machine by any available method. Whether an operator acknowledged the alarm, and what was done or not done afterwards, is unknown. The school's case is not that someone failed to switch the system off. It is that no one can say whether anyone was asked to.

The readings are not mutually exclusive, and each is stronger than the others in a part of the record. The first is the strongest on cause, since a fracture in the wet zone at the time the potential was driven below the hydrogen line is exactly what the mechanism predicts; the second is the strongest on the flaw, since some of the wires would have failed in time; the third is the strongest on what the people knew. The three would be reconciled by one thing the record does not carry: the order in which the wires broke. Rust has kept the shape of every fracture and no record of its date.

## What the number can bear

The counter's figure cannot be read as a population. The turnstile counted one stair of three, in one direction, and a person who left by it was not subtracted. The structure was opened as a storm refuge at the municipality's discretion in at least eleven recorded floods, in each of which entry through the two uncounted ramp doors was the larger route, because the stair was at the low end of the deck. The count of 11,461 or 11,462 is therefore a lower bound on forty-two years of admissions to a building whose posted capacity is 2,400, and carries no information at all about the number of persons beneath the slab when it parted, or whether there were any. Whether a flood-year admission in +62 pT (2007 CE) took place is not in the record: the municipality's order of admission, which was a paper form, is lost with the rest of the municipal file.

The Faculty's convention for the situation is to publish the bound and decline the inference. The bound is the figure on the wheels. The wheel that would add one or leave it as it was is held by a crust of green basic chloride, in the same wall, from the same water, in the same days.

## Provenance and ground

The counter and its wall section rest on the Institute for Deep Record Studies' +1,020 pT (2965 CE) resurvey of the Lowmarsh basin and its neutron-tomographic scans; the tendon ghosts on computed tomography of seven recovered blocks; the controller logic on a firmware repository in the Code Deposit; the eleven days on the intercepted modem traffic in the Wasatch Corpus, as read by Okonkwo, D. The wet-zone clustering is Ibarra, N.'s; the void geometry is Halvorsen, E.'s; the classification of the log entries is Marchetti, L.'s. No municipal file, register of admission, or contemporary report of casualties or of their absence survives. The structure's name, which would have been cut in the blank panel above the stair lintel, was never cut, or was cut in a medium that did not last; the Faculty designates it by the basin alone.

## Notes

The chemistry of passivity and of chloride and carbonation attack on steel in concrete, Faraday's law, the volume expansion of rust, the pitting of prestressing wire at grout voids, hydrogen evolution potentials in alkaline electrolyte, hydrogen embrittlement of high-strength steel, selective dealloying of brasses, and basic copper chloride corrosion of bronze are established science and engineering of the period; the structure, its controller, the flood, and the counter's service history are reconstructed. The 19 megacoulombs and 98 moles are derived from the stated 20 amperes over eleven days at the Faraday constant, as upper bounds.
