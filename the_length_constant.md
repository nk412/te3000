# The Length Constant

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from intercepted submarine-cable supervisory telemetry held in the Wasatch Corpus, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Length Constant is the Historical Faculty's working name for an unlabelled cable-landfall hut on a delta coast, whose own line-fault kit was, a lifetime after the cable it served had stopped carrying traffic, turned unmodified on the nerves of the people sheltering inside it.** The hut's engineering telemetry survives complete, intercepted decades before anyone read it; what the kit found in its second use survives only inside that same telemetry, typed into fields never built to hold it.

---

Driven into the levee clay outside the threshold, a length of the hut's own crush-wire — the galvanised steel armour stripped from a metre of dead cable — is bent by hand into five characters: NANDAR. Nothing recovered from the site, or from the traffic it once carried, explains who bore the name, when, or how long the marker has stood there; it is the one word at the site written by a person for a person, in a place whose every other surviving word was written by, or for, a machine. The hut itself carries no such name. It is a landfall station — the ninth of fourteen along a regional trunk route stitched across the Bay of Bengal and the Andaman Sea — built to bring a fibre-optic cable ashore, monitor it, and otherwise be ignored, and for most of its working life it was. It stopped being ignored twice: once when the coast under it began to go, and once, a lifetime later, when people walking inland through the wreck of it found its diagnostic kit still worked, and asked it a question it had never been built to answer but could, it turned out, already answer.

## Landfall

The hut went up in +58 pT (2003 CE), the point where an offshore cable came ashore, was jointed to a land line, and handed its traffic on. Its own job was narrow: run an optical supervisory channel down the fibre, measure the light returning off each repeater and off the beach joint, and report loss, in decibels per kilometre, against a baseline set at commissioning. A subsidence survey redone in +115 pT (2060 CE) gave the site fifteen years before the delta's own compaction and the rising mean sea level put its floor below the highest ordinary tide. Storm surge in +121 pT (2066 CE) and again in +129 pT (2074 CE) took the fifteen years down to five. By +134 pT (2079 CE) the cable's shore end had been spliced onto a longer duct and drawn back onto higher ground half a kilometre inland; the original hut, its slab already undermined, was left standing, unpowered from the grid but not unpowered altogether — a solar trickle charge kept its supervisory transceiver reporting into an otherwise empty building for reasons no surviving instruction states. Nobody came back to shut it off.

## Decrement

The inland duct followed the same low ridge — built up over decades of dredging spoil from the cable's own trench — that the region's displaced were, by then, already using as a dry path off the flooding coast; the delta's minor roads went under most rainy seasons after +140 pT (2085 CE), and the ridge did not. By +205 pT (2150 CE) the abandoned hut, still dry, still faintly powered, had become a known stop on a route that had no name of its own either, just a direction: up and away from the water. An outbreak of diphtheria in +208 pT (2153 CE), moving with the same crowding and broken supply lines that made the road necessary in the first place, left a share of survivors with the disease's characteristic late complication — a demyelinating peripheral neuropathy, appearing weeks after the original illness, in which the bacterium's toxin halts protein synthesis in the Schwann cells that build and maintain the insulating sheath around a peripheral nerve fibre. Wherever that sheath is lost, saltatory conduction — the fast, node-to-node hop the sheath makes possible — fails, and the fibre falls back to a slower, continuous, decremental mode of signalling: a voltage injected at one point leaks away along the membrane as it travels, exactly as it does down any imperfectly insulated conductor.

One of the people on the road had trained, years before, as a splicer and line technician on cables built like the one still buried under the ridge; among the little that person carried was a handheld test set built to find faults in a cable pair by injecting a signal and reading how much of it came back. Faced with patients who could not feel a pinprick past the wrist or ankle and could not say why, the technician wired the set's test leads to a pair of surface electrodes and measured them the only way the instrument knew how to measure anything: signal in, signal out, loss expressed in decibels down the length between two points. It worked, in the specific sense that the loss it reported tracked, patient to patient, with how badly a nerve had lost its sheath — because the equation the instrument had been built to solve, a linear equation for a resistive conductor leaking current transversely through an imperfect insulator along its length, first solved in 1855 for a transatlantic telegraph cable and generalised by later telegraph engineers to include exactly that transverse leakage, is the same equation adopted by the mid-twentieth century to describe voltage spreading passively along the membrane of a demyelinated nerve fibre. Nobody had told the instrument this. It had no way to know it was being asked the question it had already been built to answer.

## Span Fourteen

The test set's firmware offered exactly one report format, unmodified since commissioning: a location, a numbered span, a loss figure, and a flag against one of sixteen preset fault codes, auto-composed into a terse declarative line and appended to a running log the unit had kept, without a gap, since +58 pT. The technician kept using that file. Patients acquired span numbers instead of names, in sequence, and each entry the instrument wrote for itself sits beside entries the technician typed by hand into the same fields, in the same clipped grammar, on the same timestamp column — to the point that later readers cannot always say, line by line, which sentences were composed by a person and which by the reporting routine, a distinction the site's telemetry never registers and was never built to register. One line, from the log's fortieth week, reads whole: *"0403 LOCAL — SPAN 14, EAST PAIR: LOSS 41dB, THRESHOLD EXCEEDED, FAULT LIKELY. — pt now walking without stick, told him so, wrote it here bc nowhere else to write it."* Marchetti, L., whose standing caution is against reading any single Combustion-Age software routine as deliberate literary composition, cites this file as the harder case: here the routine and the person are, demonstrably, writing the same document, and nothing in the record separates their two hands.

The set's battery gauge, itself logged automatically every cycle, crosses its final recorded threshold and stops reporting at 02:57 on 16 March +213 pT (2158 CE).

## Provenance and ground

The hut's telemetry survives because its supervisory channel shared a backhaul link with a regional carrier's billing traffic, bulk-intercepted under a since-broken cryptographic assumption and read out, whole, only after that assumption failed; it is one more skimmed strand of the **Wasatch Corpus**. Okonkwo, D. reads the span log as exactly the kind of accidental record the discipline depends on: a vital register nobody intended to keep, hiding inside a maintenance file nobody thought worth deleting. The grave marker was not recovered from any archive; it was photographed in place during the same site survey that recovered the hut's structure, and NANDAR's identity is established by nothing else in the record.
