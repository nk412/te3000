The Sart Saddle Lattice

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a single fired stoneware plate, intact and error-free, engraved with a 192 × 192 cell lattice, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Sart Saddle Lattice is the Historical Faculty’s name for an early-Consolidation stoneware plate that stored, in an interleaved error-correcting code, a register of 3,791 household slots drawn up at an upland muster, and whose coding preserved the sizes of some absent households and by its own arithmetic could not preserve the sizes of others. The plate is physical record and decodes without a single damaged cell; who the households were, and whether the absent ones ever arrived, is reconstruction, and the code marks the limit of what can be reconstructed.

The plate was recovered in 2938 CE from a wall niche at 46°27′ N, 10°02′ E, 2,304 m above the Middle Combustion datum, on a saddle between two drainages that the Faculty calls Sart from a placename in the descended dialect. The Sart Saddle Lattice is a 300 mm square of vitrified stoneware, 14 mm thick and 2.9 kg, engraved with 36,864 binary cells in eighteen interleaved Reed–Solomon codewords, of which the first holds a header and the other seventeen hold a household register fixed at a muster in +209 pT (2154 CE). It was fired in +212 pT (2157 CE). It has no cracks, no spalls, and a raw cell error count of zero. It is the most legible object the Faculty holds from the first generation of the Consolidation, and its legibility is the difficulty: every cell reads, and what the cells state is a list of numbers, no name among them, whose blank positions are the only place the makers’ meaning shows. A blank position on the plate is called an erasure, and the plate has 523 of them. Of the households they stand for, the code restores 102; for 421 it can restore nothing, and which households fall on which side of that line was set by a queue ordered by what a household could pay. The mean household size read off the register, 4.07 persons, was carried into upland demographic tables for three decades as a figure for the upland. It is a figure for the muster.

The muster

The register was compiled at an upland assembly point above the last wet-cropped terrace on the saddle, by a body the plate’s header identifies only by a numeric code. The people it listed were, by the evidence of enamel from the saddle cemetery (see The Enamel Assay), a mix of lowland-raised and valley-raised adults arriving over two generations. The muster’s rule of entry is inferred, not stated: a household was admitted if it lodged a share, paid in threshed grain by the sack, against a fixed address in the lowland from which it had set out. The lodging share bought a slot in the register and, by the same act, a place in the queue for winter stores at the saddle. Slots were assigned in order of payment. The order is the plate’s only stated hierarchy, and it is a wealth order in the strict sense: the households that could put grain down first are at the head of the list, and the households that could put grain down last, or on credit, are at its tail.

Each household is one byte. The high nibble is a kin-line class, one of sixteen values, of which the Faculty has assigned meanings to eleven. The low nibble is a head-count. A slot therefore holds a class and a number, and neither a name, nor a place, nor a date. The makers had a grain-weighed, address-bound, arrival-ordered list of people, and they wrote it as arithmetic.

The lattice

Stoneware was chosen for the reasons the Hallstatt tablets were fired: a body vitrified at about 1,250–1,280 °C is glassy, absorbs almost no water, and does not rot. The cells are 1.5 mm squares, 2.25 mm² each, engraved into the leather-hard body and filled with an iron-oxide slip that fires black against a pale body. The 192 × 192 grid holds 36,864 cells. Eighteen codewords of 255 bytes take 36,720 of them; the remaining 144, a single 12 × 12 corner block, carry the orientation mark.

The code is a Reed–Solomon code over the field of 256 elements, built on the primitive polynomial x⁸ + x⁴ + x³ + x² + 1 (0x11D), with 223 data bytes and 32 parity bytes per codeword: a rate of 223/255, or 0.8745. The parameters are recorded on the plate in its own header, in the fixed-field notation of the period’s public routines:

n=255 k=223 f=32 p=0x11d b=0 d=18
e[02..18]=0,0,0,1,2,3,6,9,12,17,22,30,41,58,79,104,139

The first line is the code’s declaration. The field names match the argument list of a routine in the Code Deposit’s oldest coding libraries, and the Faculty, following Marchetti, L., draws no lineage from the match: a code with these parameters is what the parameters produce. The second line is the plate’s own. It is the reason the plate is discussed at all.

The properties of the code are those of the mathematics and are the same in any age. A Reed–Solomon code of length n and dimension k has minimum distance n − k + 1, which is the most any code of those parameters can have (the Singleton bound; such codes are called maximum-distance-separable). Any 223 of the 255 bytes of a codeword determine the other 32. The code corrects t = 16 byte errors at unknown positions, or 32 erasures at known positions, or any mixture obeying 2t + e ≤ 32: an erasure costs half of what an error costs because its location is given, so the decoder need not spend parity finding it. The decoder that solves the general case is the Berlekamp–Massey procedure to find the error locator, followed by Forney’s formula for the magnitudes; the erasure locator is known and enters as a starting polynomial.

The eighteen codewords are interleaved byte by byte across the lattice, so that a contiguous run of B bytes on the plate contributes at most ⌈B/18⌉ bytes to any one codeword. A run of up to 288 bytes — 2,304 cells, a patch 72 mm on a side, more than a twentieth of the plate — is correctable regardless of position, because stoneware fails by spalling and chipping, which is patchy and contiguous, and the makers evidently interleaved against it. Against independent bit errors the plate is less robust than its bulk suggests: the guaranteed correction of 16 bytes in 255 is a byte error rate of 6.3%, which corresponds to a bit error rate near 0.8% if the errors are scattered. The Shannon limit for a binary symmetric channel at the same rate is a bit error probability of 1.7%, where the capacity 1 − H₂(p) falls to 0.8745; bounded-distance decoding of this code operates at about half of it. The makers gave up half of the theoretical limit for guarantees that are exact and a decoding procedure that is closed-form in its steps, and the plate, undamaged, never tested either.

The plate also does not compress. The head-count nibble takes values from 1 to 8 with an empirical entropy near 2.7 bits, against the 4 bits allotted, and a variable-length code would have recovered about a sixth of the register’s bytes. The separation theorem says source coding and channel coding can be done independently without asymptotic loss; it does not say a short block should be. A variable-length code loses synchrony after a single lost symbol and the loss propagates through everything after it. A fixed-width slot keeps every position independently addressable, which is the property an erasure code needs, and the makers paid the 1.3 bits per household for it.

The held places

The header’s second line lists, for each register codeword from the second to the eighteenth, a number of slots at its tail written blank. The parity for each codeword was computed over the complete list of 223 slots as it stood at the muster; the blanks were left unfilled on the plate, with their positions given by the count in the header. The reason is not stated, and is the plate’s principal inference: the compilers held the household’s slot, and computed the parity, for households that were listed at the lowland end of the road and had not yet reached the saddle by firing. The decoder treats those blanks as erasures. The plate’s own gloss for itself, from a six-byte label field whose original phonology is not recoverable, is rendered by comparative reconstruction as what is kept for the ones behind. The Faculty uses the gloss; the word is not transcribed.

Every codeword whose erasure count e satisfies e ≤ 32 gives back the blanked slots exactly. There are twelve register codewords from the second to the thirteenth with an erasure count of 30 or fewer, and together they hold 102 blanked slots, all of which the code returns: the size and kin-line class of 102 households that had not yet arrived at firing and are, by the arithmetic, known. The five codewords from the fourteenth to the eighteenth have erasure counts of 41, 58, 79, 104 and 139 — 421 blanks in all — and each is past the bound. With e above n − k, more than one codeword fits the surviving bytes; the plate does not select among them, and for 421 households it holds only that a slot existed. The tail five codewords are 1,115 slots, 29.4% of the register, and they are the 29.4% that paid last.

The code therefore held the place of an absent household exactly as long as few of its neighbours in the queue were also absent. Those who paid first and had not yet come were remembered to the byte; those who paid last were remembered as a count of empty positions. The Faculty’s term for this is the depth gradient, and it is arithmetic rather than policy: no compiler chose to keep the rich and lose the poor, and any code with 32 parity bytes and a list sorted by payment would do the same.

The first reading

The plate was first decoded in 2940 CE by a routine that assumed the blanks were damage. Halvorsen, E., whose taphonomic survey of the saddle finds had reported the niche dry and unfrozen, treated the decoder’s reports of corrected bytes as chip and spall correction and recorded the plate as recovered with minor losses, fully restored. Nine register codewords decoded cleanly under that assumption, each reporting between zero and twelve corrections. Three failed as uncorrectable. Five failed as uncorrectable in a different way. The failures were not scattered, and neither were the corrections: every corrected byte sat at the tail of its codeword, where a contiguous patch of damage, once interleaved, would have been scattered across all eighteen. That the corrections all fell at the ends, and that the ends grew longer, block by block, down the plate, was what turned the header’s second line from a padding field into a count.

The three codewords with erasure counts of 17, 22 and 30 are the ones that could only be read by knowing the erasure positions in advance: a blind decoder, which can correct at most 16 errors, gave them up, and an erasure decoder, which can correct 32, returned them. In the Faculty’s terms they are the plate’s edge cases: households that a reader needed the makers’ count to recover. They are also the households whose recovery proves the makers meant the blanks.

The mean

The recovered register has 3,268 written slots and 102 restored, 3,370 households in all with a known head-count, and 421 with none. The written slots carry a mean of 4.07 persons; the mean falls with rank, from about 4.6 persons in the second codeword to about 3.4 in the eighteenth. Ranganathan, V. published the rank gradient in 2947 CE as a finding about the muster, and it stands. In the years after the plate’s first decoding, though, the unranked mean of 4.07 was carried into upland demographic tables on the assumption that a register of the saddle’s households was a sample of the saddle’s people, and then applied to the 421 unrecoverable slots, which were counted at 4.07 each, giving 1,713 persons; eleven regional tables adopted the figure for the upland household. The muster admitted only households that held a fixed lowland address and could lodge grain against it. A household that walked out with no address to lodge against, or lodged late, is not under-sampled in the register: it is absent from it, in the same way as every household that reached the saddle by a road other than the muster’s. The 4.07 is the mean size of the households that could pay to be counted, and the 421 slots are, by the rank gradient the plate itself records, the households least like it.

The population figure the plate supports is a count of slots: 3,791 held at the muster, 3,370 with known size, 421 without. It does not support a count of persons for the upland. Nor does it say what became of the blanks. The register was written before the households reached the saddle or failed to, and no later list survives.

Readings of the 421

Three readings of the erased slots are held, and the plate discriminates between none of them. The first, the late-arrival reading, holds that the households behind the blanks reached the saddle after firing and are simply unrecorded; it predicts lowland-raised enamel in the saddle cemetery in the right proportion, and the enamel is consistent with it but cannot be dated finely enough to separate arrivals in 2157 CE from arrivals in 2190 CE. The second, the diversion reading, holds that the households took another road and settled at another site; the Faculty holds no other register to test it against. The third, associated with Zhou-Ferreira, A., holds that the households never left the lowland end, that their absence from the saddle is not evidence of loss, and that a register with 421 blanks is a register with 421 undeparted households; this reading also survives, because a blank does not say why a position is blank.

An erasure is a position known to be empty whose content is unknown; an error is a position that is filled with the wrong thing. The plate’s makers wrote 523 erasures and no errors. What they did not write is the difference between a household that died and a household that moved, because the register was drawn up before either was known.

Provenance and ground

The entry rests on the plate alone. It was recovered from a sealed wall niche in a terraced structure on the saddle, in dry sediment; Halvorsen, E. attributes the absence of cracking to the niche’s thermal mass and to the plate’s 14 mm thickness and low porosity. Radiocarbon dating is unavailable; the +209 and +212 pT dates are the plate’s own, in a header field the Faculty reads as a count of seasons from the founding of the muster, and are cross-checked against the enclosing layer. The lattice, the field arithmetic, and the erasure counts are read directly. The muster’s rule of entry, the identity of its compilers, and the fate of the 421 are not attested.
