# The Reference Body

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from the complete genome micro-engraved on a single corrosion-resistant metal disk, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Reference Body is the Historical Faculty's name for a micro-engraved metal disk carrying one whole human genome and no people — a reference sequence matching no individual who ever lived, which the discipline read for two generations as a sampled member of the displaced population before recognising it as the coordinate origin it was built to be.** The disk and its genome are bit-exact record; that it was mistaken for a person, and then was not, is the history of its reading rather than of the object.

---

**The Reference Body is not the genome of anyone who lived.** It is a single Late Combustion metal disk, one of the corrosion-resistant nickel-alloy consignment, engraved with one complete haploid human genome — roughly 3.1 billion bases in the four-letter alphabet — and a human-legible leader on how to read it. Intact and recoverable base for base, it is nonetheless silent, because a reference genome reports no person on its own: it is the shared coordinate frame against which real people were meant to be stored cheaply, as lists of their differences from it, and not one such difference-list is on the disk. The Faculty first read it as an individual, even as a representative of the moved population whose demography the record cannot otherwise recover, and later as a frame matching no meiotic body. Its engraved name does not survive as a name; rendered, its recovered meaning is "the body that is all of ours," the pronoun the inclusive *we* the living tongue keeps from low-island contact varieties. The claim in that name is the whole error.

## What the disk carries

A genome in raw sequence is close to incompressible: four bases at nearly equal frequency carry almost the full two bits per base a four-symbol alphabet allows, and real sequence falls only to about 1.9 bits, so context modelling shaves the whole reference by under a tenth. The economy is not in the reference but in everything stored *against* it. Any two human genomes agree at all but roughly a thousandth of their bases, so an individual can be written as the few million positions where they differ, and reference-based coding compresses a whole personal genome by more than ninety-nine percent — from gigabytes to some fifteen megabytes, an effective four-hundredth of a bit per base of the frame it hangs on.

| Representation | Size | Bits per base |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reference, one byte per base (plain text) | ~3.1 GB | 8 |
| Reference, two-bit packed (four-letter alphabet) | ~750 MB | 2 |
| Reference, context-modelled | ~710 MB | ~1.9 |
| One person, as differences from the reference | ~15 MB | ~0.04 |

The disk holds the top of that table and nothing below it — the frame that had to be paid for once, and none of the cheap records it exists to make cheap. It is the master copy with no orders against it.

## The person who was never on it

The reference is not a person: it is haploid, carrying one base where a living body carries two, and a mosaic, roughly seven-tenths from a single anonymous donor and the rest patched from a handful of others, none chosen for being typical but for the quality of the material they gave. No meiosis produces such a sequence and no census contains it. Ranganathan's caution that the record over-samples the curated over the sampled has its plainest object here: read as one of the displaced, the disk answered every demographic question put to it, each answer an artefact of a frame built for coding efficiency and mistaken for a draw from a population. The genome that cannot tell a person who died from a person who moved was asked to speak for a whole moved people, and it was not even one person. The correction is Marchetti's, as with a trivial routine once debunked as canonical text: the several million differences that would have made it anyone, and the however-many people who each carried their own several million, are exactly the part that was never engraved. In 2662 CE (+717 pT) the disk was catalogued in the nickel-disk register as an individual genome of the retreat; in 2704 CE (+759 pT) the entry was amended to read *reference sequence, donor not a person*.

## Provenance and ground

The entry rests on the disk itself, one of the engraved nickel-alloy consignment whose metal Halvorsen's taphonomy credits with surviving intact where every softer carrier of the same data failed. The genome is recovered base for base from the micro-engraving; the human-legible leader gives the alphabet and the coding but neither a date nor a donor, and its late-vernacular grammar — the inclusive *we* of its recovered name among them — places the engraving in the age's most-spoken variety rather than fixing its origin. The coding facts are the period's own established information theory: the near-two-bits-per-base entropy of raw sequence, the thousandth-part divergence between human genomes, and the better-than-ninety-nine-percent compression of reference-based coding. The reading of the disk as a curated reference rather than a sample follows Ranganathan on sampling bias and Marchetti on the correction of a mistaken canonical reading; that the reference is a mosaic of a few anonymous donors, most of one, is a property of how such references were built and not a claim about these donors. Who the donors were, and how many personal genomes were ever stored against this frame, are not on the disk and are not reconstructed here.

## Notes

The leader engraved on the disk provides for a reader and addresses one, in the manner of the deliberate archives of the period; what it does not provide is any statement that the sequence is a reference rather than a person, the distinction having been, to its makers, too obvious to record.
