# The Telrang Chain

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from the 8,192-day credit ledger in the flash memory of the ninth node of a high-pass radio relay line, kept to settle the cost of carrying other nodes' frames and never meant as a record of anything else, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Telrang Chain is the Historical Faculty's name — taken from the one occurrence of the name that survives, a glaze-fired legend on a porcelain stand-off — for a seventeen-node solar radio line over a ridge pass of the high interior, reconstructed from the daily credit ledger of its ninth node, the one board of the chain that did not turn to powder in the cold.** The ledger, the powder at four other sites, and the assays of five boards are record; the chain's purpose, its keepers, and the cause of any single failure are reconstruction.

---

**The Telrang Chain was a line of seventeen self-powered radio relays, built in +233 pT (2178 CE) up a ridge of the high interior from a valley market at 1,900 metres to a weather post on the pass at 4,720, which carried one short frame per node per day down the mountain and one request per day up it, and which, on the evidence of its ledger, was undone over some four and a half years of the middle +250s pT by an allotrope of tin.** Every board in the chain had been soldered with the cheapest lead-free alloy of the age, which is 99.3 per cent tin; below 13.2 °C tin is not stable as a metal, and given years in the cold the solder of at least four of the chain's nodes, and by the ledger probably eight, went over, by about a quarter in volume, into a grey semiconducting powder that took the copper traces with it. The ninth node, at 3,310 metres, was soldered with tin and lead, and nothing happened to it. Its ledger of forwarded frames, 8,192 lines long and held in a memory chip kept cold for five and a half centuries, records the eight failures in order, then records the market going on asking for the pass every day for 6,397 days after there was no pass to ask. The ledger is the most exact record that survives of an infrastructure dying of its own alloy, and the only one that was kept by the dying.

## The allotrope

Tin has two forms at ordinary pressure. The metal, white or beta tin, is body-centred tetragonal, each atom with four near and two slightly farther neighbours, density 7.3 grams per cubic centimetre, ductile, a conductor. Grey or alpha tin is the diamond cubic structure of silicon and germanium, each atom with four neighbours in tetrahedral bonds, density 5.77, brittle, a semiconductor with a band gap of about eighty millielectronvolts. The two are in equilibrium at 286.35 kelvin, 13.2 °C, and below it grey is the lower free energy. The difference is small. The enthalpy of the change from grey to white is some 2.1 kilojoules per mole, and the driving force for white to go grey, which scales as that enthalpy times the undercooling over the equilibrium temperature, is at −30 °C about 320 joules per mole, against 2,020 joules per mole for the thermal energy *RT* at the same temperature. The reaction is driven by a sixth of the energy of the atoms' own motion; it is slowed by needing a nucleus, a first patch of grey in the white, to grow from, and it is fastest not where the driving force is largest but where the cold has not yet frozen the diffusion that builds the nucleus, which the age's measurements put near −30 °C. In pure tin without a seed the incubation at −18 °C ran to years. With a speck of grey tin laid on the surface, it runs in weeks.

The volume change is what makes it a failure and not a curiosity. White tin becoming grey swells by about a quarter, in a joint that is a thin fillet bonded on both sides to copper, and the fillet cracks, then crumbles, and the grey phase being brittle and the fresh surface being, in its turn, a seed, the transformation spreads from the crack through the metal. The age named the effect for its look, tin pest, and had described it in its Early Combustion centuries from organ pipes in unheated churches and tin ware in cold stores. It had also settled the remedy, which is not to make the joint of tin: lead in the alloy suppresses the change, as do bismuth and antimony in small quantities, and the age's long use of tin-lead solder in cold service shows no case. The age then removed the lead from its solder, for reasons that are among the best-documented of its decisions, and replaced it with alloys that were nearly all tin. The better ones carried silver, bismuth, or antimony enough to protect them. The cheapest, the near-eutectic tin-copper that the age's wave-soldering lines ran for its volume goods, carried 0.7 per cent copper and no other suppressant, and its literature said so in a footnote to its own datasheet. It was cheap. The Consolidation's board-makers, rebuilding electronics out of reclaimed tin and the age's solder stock, used the alloy that was in the sack.

## The line up the pass

The chain's name is the only datum about its builders that the record carries on its own account. It appears once, in cobalt under the glaze of a porcelain stand-off that holds the ninth node's antenna feed away from the enclosure wall, as TELRANG 9 in capitals; every other label the nodes bore, printed on polymer or painted on aluminium, is gone. The legend is a maker's mark of a kind the age's electrical porcelain carried for a thousand years, and it is the ninth node's number that identifies the chain. Telrang is not a word in any record of the interior, and the Faculty has no reading of it.

The line's geometry is the Faculty's reconstruction from the ninth node's ledger and from ground survey. Seventeen nodes stood along about seventy-four kilometres of ridge, a node every four to five kilometres in line of sight and each able to hear, over sub-gigahertz links of the age, its neighbours up to three places either side. Each node was a small board, a solar panel of a few watts, a primary lithium cell chosen for its cold performance, and a radio, in a cast aluminium enclosure with a silicone gasket, on a mast. Each carried a snow-depth and wind sensor and sent a frame of its readings every day; each forwarded the frames of the nodes above it, and the market's request frame going up. By the standard lapse rate of 6.5 kelvin per kilometre, applied to a valley annual mean of 11 °C, the mean annual temperature falls from 11 °C at the first node to 1.8 °C at the ninth and −7.3 °C at the seventeenth, and the winter nights at the upper hops fall far into the range where tin transforms fastest. That derivation is the Faculty's, from a single valley figure that the market's own gauge supplied through the same ledger; the nodes carried no thermometer that survives.

The ledger exists because the line was a market. A frame forwarded was a unit of work, and the chain's keepers, whoever they were, had built it so that each node carried a signed payment stub in the frame, one credit for each hop carried, drawn on the account of whoever had sent it. The ninth node kept a line for every day: the number of frames it had forwarded for others, the stubs' running total, the identity of the upstream nodes it had heard that day as an eight-bit mask for nodes ten to seventeen, and the sending counter on the last request stub to pass downward, which comes from the market's poller and increments once for each request. The nodes of the age's mesh systems often kept such a book. This is the one that was read.

## Eight bits

At the first line of the surviving ring, which is day 6,419 of the chain, the mask for nodes ten to seventeen reads full: eight upstream nodes heard, eight frames forwarded for them every day, one request forwarded up. The day is in +250 pT (2195 CE), seventeen and a half years into service. The ring holds only its last 8,192 days; everything before it was overwritten, and the Faculty has no record of the chain's first seventeen years except that it was running.

The first bit clears on line 212, which is day 6,630, +251 pT (2196 CE), and the bit is node fifteen. The order after it, by ring line, is node seventeen at 340; node twelve at 611; node sixteen at 702; node fourteen at 903; node thirteen at 1,244; node eleven at 1,520; and node ten at 1,795, day 8,213, +255 pT (2200 CE), after which the mask reads empty. The eight changes together span 1,583 lines, four years and four months. Seven fall on lines that verify. The eighth, node eleven's, falls within two days of a line that does not, and is bounded and not fixed.

Two schools read the order. The thermal reading holds that the nodes failed in the sequence of their cold. Nucleation in solder is a matter of time spent in the susceptible range, and the upper nodes spent more of it; the order by elevation would be seventeen, sixteen, fifteen, down to ten. The observed order has a rank correlation of 0.76 against that expectation over the eight, and the age's own tables put 0.74 as the value that eight rankings reach by chance one time in twenty. The Faculty notes that the school's strongest evidence is the one it cannot count, which is that nodes ten and eleven, the two lowest, are the last to fail and stand to reason as the warmest. The lot reading holds that the order was the order of the boards in a shipping crate. A board-maker draws solder from a sack, a sack is filled from a heap, and a heap with a contaminated shovelful of grey tin in it seeds every board it solders; the cluster of fifteen and seventeen in the first 340 lines, ahead of sixteen, is what such a lot, drawn from the same sack, would look like, and the thermal school's ranking would then be partly a coincidence of two things at once. Neither school can be put down, because the boards carry no serial numbers that survive, and eight points is too few to carry either argument.

The third objection is Zhou-Ferreira's, and is about the mask itself. A bit that clears means only that the ninth node ceased to hear that origin; a node alive but cut off, with its neighbours dead on both sides of it, looks identical. The ledger records the loss of a path eight times. Powder was found at four sites — twelve, fifteen, sixteen and seventeen — and at none of the other four sites surveyed was a board found at all. Whether ten, eleven, thirteen and fourteen died, or were cut off, or were switched off by a keeper who came up the pass one autumn and had no reason to say so, the ledger does not say.

## The asking

The mask is empty from line 1,795. The request column is not. Every day the market's poller sent its request up the mountain, and every day the ninth node received it, forwarded it to a neighbour it could no longer hear, was paid its credit, and wrote the line. The stub carries the poller's counter, which advances by exactly one per request over the whole ring: there is no skipped number, no repeat, no gap across 8,192 lines, so the Faculty reads the poller as one device that never missed a day. The credits the node earned in the window total 15,519. Of them 7,327 are the pass frames, which is the sum of the eight failure lines and is a check on them, and 8,192 are requests. 6,397 are requests made after the last pass frame passed down the mountain.

The poller had no rule for a pass that did not answer. The age's logistics systems were, as the Faculty uses the term, written to retry; there was an interval, a number of attempts, and a path to an operator, and the Faculty's guess, which has no ledger behind it, is that the last of those was empty. The ninth node's credit total is the cost of the market's asking, paid in full. The market's own account at the other end of the line is not recovered; the ledger cannot say whether the credits were drawn or only recorded.

## The ninth board

The ninth board is the only board of the chain, of the five assayed, built with tin-lead solder, and the record does not say why. The five were examined with X-ray fluorescence on the joints that survived: at sites twelve, fifteen, sixteen and seventeen, the residues on the copper pads are tin with a fraction of a per cent of copper, and no lead; on the ninth board every joint is the tin-lead eutectic, 61 to 64 per cent tin, which melts at 183 °C and has no grey form to turn into. The board was not reworked. The joints are of one alloy and one thermal history. Ochieng-Halloran, in the paper that gave the board its attention, called the cause shelving, on the argument that tin-lead stock in the Consolidation was not made but kept, in small lots, for the repair of older instruments, and that a board assembled out of a repair store's reel and not the sack is the cheapest account of a lone board in the chain. It is not a finding. No record names the store.

The flash chip in which the ledger was kept is a floating-gate memory, which holds a bit as charge trapped in an insulated gate, and which the age rated for retention in decades at 85 °C. Charge leaks off at a rate that rises with temperature in the manner of a thermally activated process, with an activation energy near one electron-volt; the datasheet's own law, extrapolated from 85 °C to the ninth node's mean of 1.8 °C, lengthens a ten-year rating by some four orders of magnitude. The extrapolation is the Faculty's, outside the range the age measured, and is offered as the reason the chip held and not as a prediction. The chip's losses are the evidence for it. Of the 8,192 lines, 7,731 verify their sixteen-bit check, and the 461 that fail are concentrated in the oldest third of the ring, the lines that were written first and have been holding their charge longest. The cold that destroyed eight nodes of the chain is the cold that kept the ninth one's book.

The ledger stops at line 8,192, on day 14,610 of the chain, +273 pT (2218 CE). The enclosure was found by the Institute for Deep Record Studies' high-interior survey in +842 pT (2787 CE), half under scree, its gasket whole and its interior dry; the board is intact, the cell is flat, and nothing in the ledger says why the node stopped. The last line carries one forwarded request, with the poller's counter at 14,610. The market had been asking for 14,610 days, and the record has no answer in it.

## Provenance and ground

The ledger was read from the ninth board's flash by chip-off imaging in +843 pT (2788 CE); its line format, check polynomial, and field names are inferred from the firmware image in the same chip, and the stubs' signatures verify under the poller's public key as recorded in the image. The powder at sites twelve, fifteen, sixteen and seventeen was identified as alpha tin by its diffraction pattern, the cubic (111) reflection at a spacing of 3.75 ångströms; Halvorsen's taphonomy of tin accounts for why a metal that survives six centuries as a cast ingot does not survive one cold decade as a joint (*The Grey Phase in the Field*, Journal of Anthropogenic Stratigraphy, 2794). Ochieng-Halloran, B. derived the failure order and its rank statistics from the mask, and assayed the five boards (*One Reel, One Board: Lead on the Telrang Pass*, Journal of Anthropogenic Stratigraphy 58(3), 2796). Okonkwo's reading of the mask as a census of whatever the node could hear, taken for the first time as a record of the hearing and not of the heard, is what makes the ledger usable. The solder compositions, the allotropes' structures, densities, enthalpy, and transformation kinetics, the lapse rate, the activation-energy law for charge loss, and the diffraction spacing are as the age measured them; the line's length, spacing, purpose, and keepers are inference from the ledger, and thin.

## Notes

1. All dates are post-Trinity; CE = pT + 1945. Ring days are counted from the node's own first boot, taken as commissioning in +233 pT (2178 CE); the node's counter was a count of daily rollovers, and the Faculty has not tested it against any clock.
2. The mean annual temperatures are the Faculty's arithmetic on one valley figure and a standard lapse rate, with no allowance for aspect, snow cover, or inversion; the nodes' own enclosures sat warmer than the air by daytime solar gain and colder by night radiation, and the net is not known.
3. The rank correlation is Spearman's, with the sum of squared rank differences 20 over eight items; the critical value of 0.738 is the age's table for a two-sided test at five per cent. It rests on the assumption that expected order follows elevation alone.
