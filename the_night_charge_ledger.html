The Night-Charge Ledger

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a bound consignment of struck nickel inspection plates and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Night-Charge Ledger is the Historical Faculty’s name for eleven quarterly inspection dockets of an ice-bank refrigeration plant on the Gulf’s southern littoral, struck onto nickel to satisfy a regional pressure-vessel code and recovered from the anoxic mud beneath its drowned municipal archive. What the plates carry — charge tonnage, condensing readings, hours of discharge — is record; what the plant’s ordinary, unrecorded weeks looked like is the reconstruction the plates cannot themselves supply.

The Night-Charge Ledger is not a diary of a plant in ordinary operation, though it was read as one for the first four decades after its recovery. It is eleven scheduled inspections of a plant built on one simple asymmetry: a bank of ice is not, in the plainest engineering sense, a way of storing cold, but a way of storing the one thing a refrigeration cycle cannot do without — a favourable temperature difference — banked at night for spending by day. Between roughly +136 and +171 pT (2081–2116 CE), a small ice-bank plant on the Gulf’s southern littoral froze water through the cooler hours of the night and melted it through the hotter hours of the day, holding a fixed reserve of cooling against a compressor that ran best when the outside air was coolest and was needed most exactly when it ran worst. What survives of the plant is not its daily record, which the Attrition took along with every other paper log on the site, but these eleven quarterly dockets, struck onto nickel under a pressure-vessel code — a curated, best-behaved slice of the plant’s life mistaken, at first, for its ordinary one.

I. The plant, running

The plant held two loops. A packaged ammonia compressor train — the plant’s single point of failure in Ibarra’s diagnostic sense, one condenser bank and one suction header, no redundant train behind either — ran through the coolest six to eight hours of the night, its evaporator coil buried in a tank of water and driven to roughly −8 °C, cold enough to freeze the tank from the coil outward at a workable rate. By morning the tank held a fixed mass of ice; through the day, warm return water from the town’s clinic and its public cooling hall was pumped across the bank and back out chilled, melting ice instead of running the compressor against the day’s own heat.

Ice over a tank of chilled water was not an aesthetic choice. Water’s latent heat of fusion is 334 kilojoules per kilogram; its specific heat is 4.186 kilojoules per kilogram-kelvin. A chilled-water tank working across a serviceable ten-kelvin range between supply and return stores about 42 kilojoules per kilogram it holds; the same kilogram, frozen and later melted across that width, stores some eight times as much. A tank sized to hold a working day’s cooling as chilled water alone would have needed to be roughly eight times the volume — space the plant’s site, a converted cold-store building, did not have. Ice was the only choice that fit the footprint.

The plant’s real constraint was never the tank; it was the night. An evaporative condenser rejects a compressor’s heat by evaporating water into passing air, and by the physics of wet-bulb depression its condensing temperature cannot be driven below the air’s own wet-bulb reading — the plant’s commissioning record rates its condenser at an eight-Celsius-degree approach above it. A compressor’s coefficient of performance, bounded above by the Carnot limit T_evap ⁄ (T_cond − T_evap) reckoned from absolute zero, falls as the condensing temperature it must reject against climbs. The docket’s own quarterly readings show the wet-bulb figure the plant was built against had nowhere to go but up — a fact this record puts to no more dramatic use than an evaporative condenser’s design margin; the survivability threshold attested elsewhere in the record is a different number entirely, and this plant’s town never crossed it in the years the plate covers.

II. The docket

Eleven quarters survive, each struck to a single nickel plate after an inspection visit scheduled and announced weeks ahead — a condition the regional pressure-vessel code required of any plant holding more than about four and a half tonnes of refrigerant charge, adopted after an unrelated ammonia release elsewhere had made permanent, corrosion-resistant retention a standing rule. Each plate carries the inspector’s readings for that one week: overnight wet-bulb temperature at the start of charging, the plant’s estimated cycle coefficient of performance (computed from the readings above at roughly fifty-two per cent of the theoretical Carnot limit, consistent with industrial ammonia refrigeration generally), the tonnage of ice laid down, and the hours of discharge delivered to each of the plant’s two service classes.

Class A was the plant’s paid subscriber loop — the clinic and a handful of metered offices on continuous feed. Class C was the public cooling hall, supplied on whatever the tank had left after Class A’s draw, and its column is the one in this table that moves in only one direction. The subscriber accounts behind Class A survive only as numbers, but the households named against those numbers in the region’s later dental-isotope survey are, disproportionately, the households recorded as having left the littoral early — the wealth tell already familiar from the age’s larger displacement, in miniature: the means that bought a household a place on the continuous feed bought it, more often than not, a place on the road out while the road was still open. Class C’s hours did not fall because the hall’s users needed cooling less. They fell because they were, structurally, whoever was left drawing on whatever the paying loop did not use first.

For four decades after the plates’ recovery the Faculty read this table as the plant’s typical year, and Ranganathan’s later reassessment of the deposit gives the reason flatly: an inspection scheduled weeks ahead is not a week a plant’s operators let run as any other week did. The docket the plate preserves is not a sample of the plant’s life. It is the plant’s best week, struck four times a year because the code demanded it, and kept as the record because it was the only week committed to a medium the Attrition could not take.

III. What the plate does not carry

The plant’s own name is not recovered. No plate carries it — the code required inspection data, not a letterhead — and the daily log that might have named it, kept on paper in the same building, did not survive the flood that later put the archive under anoxic mud. Halvorsen’s taphonomic reading of the deposit is unambiguous about why one medium lived and the other did not: nickel does not need to stay dry.

Nor does any plate carry a week that was not an inspection week, which the docket’s own trend — Class A intact to the last full quarter, Class C erased three quarters before it — leaves the Faculty no way to soften. The docket for +172 pT (2117 CE) was never struck.

Provenance and ground

Eleven struck nickel plates, roughly postcard-sized, recovered from a drowned municipal building’s basement archive on the Gulf littoral, in anoxic mud that preserved the plates’ engraving and nothing organic in the same room. The plates are legible in full and internally consistent in units and format across the run, indicating a single inspecting authority throughout. No paper record from the same building survives, and the plant’s compressor bay and tank room have not been physically located; the plates alone are this entry’s evidence. The reading given here — that the docket over-samples the plant’s best behaviour rather than recording its typical one — follows Ranganathan’s published reassessment of the deposit, not its first cataloguing, which took the run at face value.
