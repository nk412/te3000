The Up-Three Plate

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a Standard Terran idiom and a stamped nameplate recovered from the geosynchronous ring, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Up-Three Plate is the Faculty’s name for a stainless nameplate on a dead relay satellite in the geosynchronous ring, which fixes the origin of the Standard Terran idiom “gone up three” for those who left early and with means. The plate and the satellite’s orbit are record; the link between the plate and the idiom is reconstruction.

+46 pT (1991 CE): the second of two relay satellites sharing one longitude was left in the ring when its propellant ran out. The first had been lifted out of the operating belt two years earlier. The idiom done go up three, “has gone up three hundred,” survives in Standard Terran as the phrase for a household that relocated before the wells failed, and stay in the lane for one that did not.

The burn

A satellite in the geosynchronous ring, 42,164 km from the Earth’s centre, moves at about 3.07 km/s. To raise it 300 km, two small burns suffice, together costing about 11 m/s. Routine north–south station-keeping against lunar and solar pull costs about 50 m/s each year, so the lift cost roughly eighty days of the satellite’s working life. For a craft of two tonnes on a monopropellant of specific impulse near 220 s, the lift took about ten kilograms of propellant. The first relay received it at +44 pT (1989 CE) and was left drifting westward at a little under 4° a day, clear of the lane.

The lane

The second relay lost propellant to a leaking valve, as the Faculty reconstructs it, and had under 5 m/s remaining at its last working day. It was switched off in place. An abandoned satellite does not stay still: the Earth’s equatorial bulge draws it toward two stable longitudes, near 75° E and 105° W, and the pull of Moon and Sun tilts its orbit by about 0.85° a year until the tilt crests near 15° after some twenty-six years, a north–south swing of about 11,000 km. The craft crossed the operating belt twice every day for the quarter-century, and the plate remains there. The cost of the ten kilograms not carried was not borne by the operator, which is recorded as having dissolved before +60 pT (2005 CE). It fell to every craft that held the lane afterward.

The plate

The nameplate reads RELAY II — VALLEY DISTRICTS SERVICE — COMMISSIONED +41, and is the only place the satellite’s name survives; its telemetry does not. The service area lay on the Indo-Gangetic plain. The Faculty’s survey of the ring recovered the plate by imaging in 2744 CE. The first relay has not been found.

The idiom’s reading

One school, the Okonkwo reading, holds that “up three” was coined in the operators’ own trade and carried outward by the engineers’ families, who had the means to leave. A second, corpus-critical, holds that the phrase is a coincidence of a round number and a rising tide, and that the plate is a stray. The dialect record for the idiom begins some two centuries after the plate was stamped, and cannot be dated closer.

Provenance and ground

Idiom: attested in oral residue in Standard Terran, undated. Plate: stamped steel, legible in the Faculty’s 2744 CE survey images.
