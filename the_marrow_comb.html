The Marrow Comb

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a single nickel leaf in the language-disk format, engraved with a narrowband radio detection log and a three-line caption, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Marrow Comb is a 91-second, 1,512-line narrowband radio detection at the hydrogen line, logged in +140 pT (2085 CE) by a fixed-dish receiver and known only from one engraved nickel leaf. The frequencies, amplitudes, drift and caption are record; the source, the sender and the meaning of the line count are reconstruction, and three readings of them are held open.

The Marrow Comb takes its name from the word engraved above the first line of a nickel leaf 76 mm across; the word occurs on no other durable medium, and whether it names the receiving station, the engraver, or a place is not known. The leaf records one detection: a comb of 1,512 evenly spaced, equal-amplitude tones centred on 1420.405752 MHz, present for 91 seconds on the night of 3 October +140 pT (2085 CE) and never seen again. Its sidereal drift is that of a source fixed among the stars. Its line count sits fourteen above the entry count of the language disk. Three schools read it as a stellar signal, as a probe’s transmission from beyond 550 AU, or as a processing artefact; the leaf cannot separate them.

The leaf

The leaf is nickel, electroformed from a glass master, and its micro-engraving is legible at 1,000× as the language disk’s is. It was catalogued by the Institute for Deep Record Studies among the Nickel Disks consignment because it was found nested in a language disk’s protective sleeve; the two are separate objects, and nothing ties the leaf to the disk’s makers.

The recto carries a line list: 1,512 frequency offsets from the centre line at 0.5 Hz spacing, each with an amplitude count, all within one count of one another. The verso carries three lines of caption in a variety already showing the grammar that Standard Terran later fixed: a preverbal completive derived from “done,” and an inclusive “we.” The Faculty renders it:

Marrow comb. Ninety-one second. It done come; yumi hear.

No date, station designation, or coordinates are given. The date is recovered from the sidereal timestamp in the log header, which fixes the night to within a day.

The transit

The log states the detection lasted 91 seconds and rose and fell in a smooth, symmetric envelope. For a fixed reflector the envelope is expected: the sky drifts through the beam at 15.041 arcseconds per second of time, times the cosine of the declination. The leaf gives a declination near +10°, so 91 seconds is 1,346 arcseconds, or a beam full width of 0.374°. That corresponds to a reflector of about 33 m aperture at 21 cm, and to an effective collecting area near 430 m² at half efficiency. The envelope therefore says only that the source was fixed on the sky for the length of a transit. A star does this. So does anything else that moves less than a beamwidth in a minute and a half.

The drift agrees. The Doppler acceleration a fixed site feels from the Earth’s rotation, at the latitude the header implies, is ω²R cos φ cos δ, and the plate’s rate falls inside the combined rotational and orbital terms.

A transmitter on the ground co-rotates with the receiver and shows no such drift. A drift at the rotational rate says the source was not on the Earth. It does not say how far off.

Three readings

The stellar reading (Ashworth-Nakamura) takes the drift and the transit at face value: a transmitter at stellar distance, on a fixed bearing. Its strongest form is that no other hypothesis is needed. Its weakness is the power budget. From the engraved flux of 2.4 × 10⁻²³ W m⁻² in a 1 Hz bin, and a system temperature of 25 K, a source at 40 light-years must radiate an effective 43 TW isotropically, or 81 GW into a 2 m dish. The largest ground planetary radar of the age radiated about twenty terawatts effective.

The transponder reading (Okonkwo) places the source beyond the Sun’s gravitational focus, 547.6 AU out and beyond, where a probe of the Late Combustion programmes was proposed to sit. A source at 600 AU shows the same rotational drift as a star, since a parallax of 344 arcseconds accrues only over months. It needs only 4.6 kW into a 2 m dish. It fits the plate, and it fits the age’s own plans. Its weakness is that no transmitter, uplink log or launch record survives; the round-trip light-time of 6.93 days to 600 AU means an uplink would have preceded the detection by a week, and the array’s log keeps nothing from that week. The gravitational lens itself gives no help here: the ideal gain, 4π²r_g/λ with r_g = 2.953 km, would be 5.5 × 10⁵ at this wavelength, but coronal plasma refracts radio rays too far for the focus to hold.

The artefact reading (Halvorsen) holds that the comb is a product of the receiver’s own chain: an evenly spaced comb is what a sampling clock and its harmonics produce, and an apparent drift is what a de-Doppler stage adds to a fixed spur. The log does not state whether its drift is raw or corrected. The two differ in sign. The leaf’s drift agrees with a celestial source only if it is raw; if corrected, the sign is reversed and the reading survives. The 91 seconds and the envelope would be the tracking transit of a pipeline that had been set to a fixed bearing. Its weakness is that spurs of this kind repeat, and the Comb is recorded once.

None of the three can be discriminated by the one thing that would discriminate them, a second detection.

The line count

The leaf’s 1,512 lines were compared with the language disk’s catalogue in the survey of +682 pT (2627 CE), and found to number fourteen more than the disk’s 1,498 entries. The survey reported the match as evidence that the sender had drawn on the world’s languages, and the flat amplitude was read as a flat census: one line per tongue, each of equal weight. The disk gave one entry to each language whatever the number of its speakers, so a tongue of forty speakers stands beside one of a billion, and the flat comb inherits the flatness of the list. The disk is a curated record, and the matching count was taken for a sample of the age’s languages for some two hundred and fifty years. The fourteen extra lines have no counterpart in any surviving list.

Provenance and ground

The leaf is held in the Institute for Deep Record Studies, catalogued with the nickel disks. Ranganathan’s caution on sampling applies to the disk, not the leaf. The array that made the detection is unidentified, and no other record of the night is known.
