The Northmark Mangrove

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from thirty-three years of nursery haulage routing telemetry skimmed at a western Atlantic cable landing, and from the moulded polyethylene stock tags recovered with it from anoxic sound-side mud, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Northmark Mangrove is a cold-selected black-mangrove stock of the Late Combustion western Atlantic seaboard, known from its moulded nursery tags and from the delivery log of the fleet that carried it north, whose thirty-three-year poleward march the discipline reads three ways and cannot choose between. The tags, the drop coordinates, and the physics of frost, flotation, and accretion are record; what the march was for, and what became of the stock beyond its last logged drop, are reconstruction.

On 14 March +88 pT (2033 CE) a nursery haulage vehicle logged a drop of 4,800 mangrove propagules on a sound-side shore of the western Atlantic seaboard at 31.6° N, some sixty kilometres north of the latitude at which the age’s own botanists then placed the tree’s poleward limit. The drop is the first of 1,412 in a routing log that runs to +121 pT (2066 CE), each one moving, on average, a little further north than the last. The stock they carried was a black mangrove — Avicennia, the most frost-tolerant of the mangrove genera, buoyant-seeded, salt-excreting, the tree the age planted as a living shoreline where the sea was rising — selected over some decades for a shallower leaf-damage threshold and sold under a cultivar name that survives only on the tag. The tag reads AVG · NORTHMARK · PPAF · LOT 31-N. The log tracks the tree’s advance up the coast at the pace of the retreating frost, or of the retreating people, or of the crews who planted it; the record supports all three and adjudicates none. Where the log ends, the tree does not.

The tag and the stock

The tags are injection-moulded polyethylene, 60 by 20 mm, with a punched eyelet and raised lettering; they were tied to bundles of propagules for delivery and, by whatever custom, left on the shore. Halvorsen’s taphonomy attributes their survival to burial in the mud they were meant to make: mangrove sediment is anoxic within a few centimetres, and polyethylene in the absence of oxygen and light neither oxidises nor is eaten at any rate a millennium can measure. Two hundred and six tags have been recovered from cores along 340 km of shore, all from the same mould, all from lots numbered in a single ascending series. PPAF is the age’s abbreviation for a plant patent applied for; the application, if it was granted, was granted on paper, and the paper is gone. Nothing survives of the cultivar’s own genome, because the trees that grew from these lots stood in the intertidal, and the intertidal moved.

What the stock was is inferred from what its growers claimed for it in the payload codes, and from what the wild tree can do. Black mangrove at its poleward margin is limited not by mean temperature but by the tail: leaf damage begins close to −4 °C, tree mortality rises near −6.6 °C, and a stand caught between the two loses its canopy and grows it back within one to three winters. A cultivar advertised as cold-selected was, on the best reading, a stock in which the damage threshold had been pushed down by a degree or so. It did not need more. The coldest nights of that continent had, since +35 pT (1980 CE), warmed at roughly 2.2 times the rate of its winter mean, so that the frost line — the isoline of a hard freeze in an ordinary winter — was moving north faster than any average would suggest. A tree that could take one more degree of cold, planted one degree of latitude beyond its old limit, needed only to wait.

The line of frost

The sound was the tree’s ally. A hard freeze on that coast is a continental polar air mass advected over the shelf; the water beneath it, having taken up more than nine-tenths of the heat the age retained, gives back latent and sensible heat all night, and the minima over a broad sound run warmer than the minima a few kilometres inland. The age’s growers knew this and planted to it: nearly every drop lies within 400 m of open water, on the lee of a barrier island, in the thermal shadow of the sea. The log’s drop latitudes, plotted against the recovered winter minimum record, follow the −4 °C isoline north with a lag of two winters, as if the fleet were waiting to see the frost fail twice before trusting it.

The drops

The routing telemetry is the entry’s record. It is a dispatch system’s per-stop stream — vehicle, timestamp, coordinates, payload code, a free-text note — kept so that a fleet could be billed and its drivers audited, and skimmed with everything else at the landing station of a trans-Atlantic cable, where it lay unread in the Wasatch Corpus until the era’s public-key ciphers fell. It is, in Okonkwo’s phrase, a census nobody meant to take. The note field is where the authorship void surfaces. Early notes are plainly typed by a hand — “tide up, left above wrack, tell K” — and early system notes are plainly not. By +104 pT (2049 CE) the field carries, in the same slot, notes of a single shape:

2049-04-02T06:41Z V-118 31.98N 81.08W NM-31 x4800 NOTE: Leave propagules above the wrack line; notify K.

Whether a driver wrote it, or a dispatcher, or the routing engine wrote it for a driver from a template it had learned from him, the corpus cannot say; the discipline has stopped trying. K is not identified.

The credit rode with the payload. Each drop’s code carries a sequestration term — one hundred years — and the age’s own measurements put a mature stand at about 1.7 tonnes of carbon a hectare a year, most of it into soil, or something over six tonnes of carbon dioxide. The credit was issued at planting against the whole term; the relative sea-level rise at the first drop site was 5.4 mm a year, and on the age’s published projections for that coast it crossed seven before the fourth decade of the term, which is the rate above which the age’s own coring showed no mangrove had ever sustained accretion. The lot numbers continue.

Three readings

The march north is not in dispute; its meaning is. Three readings are held.

The frost reading (Maalouf) takes the log at its word: the stock was a shoreline-defence planting, and the fleet went north because the frost let it. The two-winter lag behind the −4 °C isoline is the whole argument, and it is a strong one — no other reading predicts the lag.

The vacancy reading (Teixeira-Bal, after Ranganathan) notes that a living shoreline is planted on land somebody owns, and that the cheapest such land is land whose owners have gone. Cross-matched against the rest of the Wasatch Corpus, the drop addresses lie, three times in four, inside clusters of purchasing telemetry that had gone quiet in the preceding five years. On this reading the fleet followed not the frost but the leavers, and the log is an accidental census of the ground the coast’s people had already given up — the tree took the shore as the people left it.

The road reading (Okonkwo) is the plainest. The vehicles’ home depots, recoverable from their overnight stops, themselves move north through the log: the crews were coastal people, and the sites are where the crews then lived. The stock went north because its planters did.

The record cannot choose. All three readings predict a northward line, and the three lines nearly coincide, because the frost, the vacancy, and the road were moving up the same coast at about the same pace. Wondering aloud in the Journal of Anthropogenic Stratigraphy, Maalouf conceded that the fleet may not have known either.

North of the last tag

The last drop is logged on 9 November +121 pT (2066 CE), at 34.7° N; the note field is empty. The northernmost recovered tag lies at 34.5° N. But black mangrove does not need a truck. Its propagules float on air trapped in the hypocotyl, lose and regain buoyancy in full seawater, stay afloat for more than a hundred days, and establish within a month of stranding; the tree is built to outrun its planters on the flood. Pollen of Avicennia appears in sound-side cores at 35.9° N in layers dated, by the fly-ash marker, to the +130s pT (2070s CE) — a latitude the −4 °C isoline had not reached when the log stops, and to which no drop was logged. Whether it is the Northmark stock or the wild line, whether the trees behind it were planted or arrived, and whether anyone was on that shore to see them come, no tissue survives to say. The cores from further north have not yet been cut.

Provenance and ground

The routing stream is a single skim of fleet-dispatch telemetry, +88 to +121 pT, read out from the Wasatch Corpus and cross-matched to its advertising telemetry under Ranganathan’s cautions on sampling: the coast it records is a purchasing, connected coast, and the log ends where the purchasing did. The tags are physical record; the payload codes and the cultivar name are read from them. The frost, flotation, accretion, and sequestration figures are the period’s own published measurements, not the discipline’s.
