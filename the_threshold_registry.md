# The Threshold Registry

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from intercepted laboratory-order telemetry in the Wasatch Corpus, cross-read against four surviving laminated specimen cards, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Threshold Registry is the Historical Faculty's name for a Middle Combustion universal newborn metabolic-screening programme, known almost entirely from the routine order-and-result traffic it generated rather than from any surviving clinical file.** The programme's existence, its scale, and its expanding disorder panel are physical record, recovered whole from intercepted network telemetry; the parents, the children, and every name a given card once carried are not.

---

At 61°04′N, 5°19′E, on the third floor of a fjord-coast public health laboratory raised on reclaimed harbour fill, a square of absorbent filter stock the size of a postal envelope was, for the better part of five decades, the entire genetic surveillance apparatus of one small nation. The Historical Faculty calls it the Threshold Registry because no clean surviving document gives its own name for itself. It began in +18 pT (1963 CE) as a single bacterial bioassay for a single recessive metabolic disorder, administered by heel-prick to every infant at forty-eight hours old, and grew, expansion by dated expansion, into a simultaneous screen for several dozen. Untreated, the founding disorder lets an amino acid accumulate in an infant's blood past what the developing brain can tolerate; caught inside the first weeks of life and managed by diet, it largely does not. The programme's own paper archive did not survive it — filter stock kept in an unclimatised provincial store does not last three thousand years, and by the read of the record does not appear to have lasted even a hundred. What survived is the traffic: the routine order-and-result stream the central laboratory exchanged with a network of rural clinics, swept up in bulk decades before anyone holding it could read a byte of it, opened at the far end of the millennium once factoring the-era's public-key ciphers stopped being hard, and cross-read against four demonstration cards a training office had, for reasons of its own, sealed in plastic. From that traffic the Faculty has recovered a running census of a national birth cohort, the outline of a founder population the network was never built to notice, and one gap the programme's own annual reports never once described as a gap.

## A card at forty-eight hours

The technique it replaced was clinical observation: a disorder announced itself, if at all, in a toddler's stalled development, by which point the damage was done. The Threshold Registry's founding act was to move the test to before symptoms existed at all. A trained attendant lanced an infant's heel, filled two printed circles on the card with capillary blood, and let it air-dry; the card, with the family's registration number but no name recoverable from the intercepted stream, was posted to the central laboratory inside a standard mailing window. There the assay itself was, for its first three decades, biological rather than instrumental: a punched disc of the dried blood spot was laid on a growth medium seeded with a strain of soil bacterium and poisoned with a synthetic amino-acid analogue that starved the culture unless enough of the natural amino acid was present in the disc to outcompete it. Blood carrying the amino acid above a fixed concentration let the bacterial colony's growth ring expand past a printed reference diameter; blood below it did not. The card, the ring, and the ruler were the whole instrument. No electronic reading was taken, no result computed — a technician measured a circle of bacterial growth against a printed template and wrote a pass or a recall by hand, a genetic diagnosis performed with tools a nineteenth-century laboratory would have recognised, decades after that same laboratory had already sequenced its first genome.

## The threshold and the coast

The disorder is autosomal recessive: two defective copies of the gene, one from each parent, are required before an infant shows any biochemical sign, and a single copy — carried by roughly one person in fifty across the mainland population, healthy, undetected, and never screened for — confers no disadvantage the culture-ring assay could see. Against that mainland background, the intercepted stream shows an anomaly the laboratory's own summaries never remarked on: from a stretch of six fishing settlements down the country's outer skerry coast, founded within two generations of each other by a few dozen families some six generations before the programme began, recall flags for the founding disorder arrive at close to twelve times the rate the mainland cards return. Read against Hardy–Weinberg proportions — the elementary rule that a recessive allele's carrier frequency and its double-recessive incidence are locked together by simple arithmetic — a homozygote incidence near one birth in nine hundred, as the coast's cards show across the recovered decades, implies a defective-allele frequency near one in thirty and a carrier frequency, among people the assay could never flag at all, close to one adult in sixteen. The mainland's one-in-eleven-thousand incidence implies a carrier frequency nearer one in fifty-three. No deliberate close-kin marriage produced the gap; a small founding population, isolated by the coast's own geography rather than by custom, simply carried a higher starting frequency of one ordinary allele than the mainland it fed into, and genetic drift in a population that size did the rest. The recall rate is not a record of a healthier or sicker people. It is a record of how few founders a lineage had, multiplied forward by arithmetic that does not care about intention.

## A panel that grew

`ORD 1963-11-02 // SITE 0442 // SPOT 1/2 // PANEL V1 // RES PHE 4.9MG/DL THRESH 4.0 // DISP RECALL-NOACK`

The founding panel tested for exactly one condition. Expansion followed the instrument, not the disease list: once a bench technique arrived in +47 pT (1992 CE) that could ionise a blood-spot extract and sort its molecular fragments by mass in a single automated run, a card that had answered one question began, without any change to how it was collected, answering thirty. By +58 pT (2003 CE) the panel screened twenty-seven distinct inherited disorders of amino acid, fatty-acid, and organic-acid metabolism from the same two dried circles that had once fed a single bacterial culture. The laboratory's own information system, an ordinary piece of decision automation and the only point in the whole pipeline where a machine's output stood unmediated between a result and a discharged family, flagged for manual review any reading above its printed threshold and filed the rest without a human eye on it. What the panel's expansion never did, and what no surviving order record shows it ever proposing to do, was reach backward. A card collected in +30 pT (1975 CE) had been tested, correctly, for the one disorder the instrument of +30 pT could detect; it was never retrieved, never re-run, and never re-read against the twenty-six additional disorders the same two blood spots — had the card itself survived storage, which by the record it generally did not — could in principle have answered decades later. The consent a parent gave at the hospital covered the single test performed that week and nothing the programme might later learn to ask the same spot. No annual report from the coast or the mainland ever logs that gap as unfinished business; each year's summary simply reports the current panel's own results, as though the disorders it could not yet see had never been part of anyone's care.

## The intercept

The order-and-result stream survives at all only because a signals programme with no interest in newborn health was recording everything that crossed a set of cable landing points, on the calculation that ciphers unbreakable in +38 pT (1983 CE) — the year the laboratory's clinic network moved its order traffic onto a shared regional data line — would not stay unbreakable forever. It was right on both counts: the traffic went out encrypted, correctly, on hardware of its era; and it stayed unread for a little over eight centuries, until a quantum-capable successor to that same signals tradition factored the keys and released the whole intercepted decade into what the discipline now catalogues as the Wasatch Corpus. What the Faculty holds is therefore not a clinical archive but an accidental one — order codes, spot counts, panel versions, threshold values, and disposition flags, none of it explained, all of it perfectly preserved, because a bulk interceptor logging cable traffic for national-security reasons in +38 pT had no reason to care what a five-character panel code meant and every reason to keep every byte of it. A margin comment on one recalled card, entered by a clinic clerk into a free-text field the ordering software never validated, survives with the rest:

> *Mother done moved with the family before the second card come back, in't nothing here to send it to, isn't it.*

The line is unremarkable to its own writer and load-bearing to the Faculty: the pre-verbal completive marking "done moved," and the invariant tag closing a statement rather than a question, are both features of the same displaced South Asian vernacular contact grammar that, carried on different tongues and mixed with an unrelated island contact grammar, becomes the ancestor of Standard Terran itself. A clinic clerk on a fjord coast, transcribing a mother's own words into a field meant for a delivery address, is recorded — without either of them knowing it — in the grammar the whole species would eventually come to speak.

## Four cards in plastic

Nothing in the intercepted stream explains its own abbreviations. PANEL V1 through V4, PHE, THRESH, DISP, and the five-digit site codes are opaque strings until matched against something that spells them out, and the working archive that could have supplied that key did not survive. What did survive, isolated in a training-office storeroom rather than the records vault, are four demonstration cards, laminated in an inert plastic sleeve some years after collection for use in staff instruction and never returned to circulation — a routine administrative act, unrelated to preservation, that happened to seal them against exactly the humidity that dissolved the working archive's paper. Each carries, printed across its header in full, the programme's own name for itself, its panel legend spelling out PHE as the analyte and THRESH as its recall cutoff in the instrument's own units, and a site code matching one of the numbers repeating through the intercepted order stream. Okonkwo's method — reading a deposit's incidental metadata, the codes and timestamps nobody thought worth explaining, as an unintended census of the population that generated them — supplies the rest: matched against the four cards' legend, the site-code frequencies across nearly six decades of intercepted orders resolve into a year-by-year national birth count no surviving demographic register from the region otherwise provides, and a regional recall-rate map that is, for one recessive disorder on one stretch of coast, the only prevalence survey the founder population ever received.

## Provenance and ground

The intercepted stream is bit-exact from the point of capture; nothing in it has been reconstructed or inferred beyond what the codes literally state. The four laminated cards are physical objects, independently dated by their own printed batch stamps, and their legend is the sole basis for reading any field in the stream as anything more specific than an opaque token. No clinical record naming a single tested infant, on the coast or the mainland, is known to survive in any archive.

## Notes

The laminated cards' survival is an ordinary case of substrate outliving intent: the sealed plastic outlasted the unsealed paper it was cut from by a margin of centuries, not years, for no reason connected to anyone's wish to preserve it. The intercepted order-and-result stream, closed out at its latest dated entry, recovers 2,861,406 individual card records; 31,844 of them carry a site code from the six-settlement coast.
