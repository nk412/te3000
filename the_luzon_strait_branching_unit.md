# The Luzon Strait Branching Unit

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from an optical supervisory and alarm telemetry stream intercepted off a leased circuit and held in the Wasatch Corpus, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **An intact but silent submarine cable branching unit on the floor of the Luzon Strait, reconstructed from the supervisory telemetry it emitted until +142 pT (2087 CE).** The telemetry, the fault report, and the traffic they frame are record; the unit's condition on the seabed is taphonomic inference, and its name rests on a single riveted plate.

---

Three thousand one hundred metres of seawater cover the unit. Emplaced at +119 pT (2064 CE) on the floor of the Luzon Strait, it split a trunk cable into one spur serving a small archipelago to the east. In the graph of the era's cable network that spur was a bridge — an edge whose removal increases the number of connected components. A cut of edge-connectivity 1 is a certainty, not a probability; no surplus capacity anywhere else can stand in for a path that does not exist. A second spur, costed and permitted at +131 pT (2076 CE), was deferred against a falling subscriber count and never funded. At +142 pT (2087 CE) the spur faulted seaward of the unit, and the archipelago's addresses left the reachable graph.

A bridge carries everything that crosses it or nothing; the right-hand column is a theorem, not a measurement, and no decline on the left could move it. Among the last complete flows the intercept preserves before the fault report are several video calls between the same two endpoint addresses, each a few minutes long, otherwise unremarkable. The telemetry knows the unit only as a physical-layer address; its name survives on one engraved titanium builder's plate riveted to the housing.

## Provenance and ground

The entry rests on a supervisory telemetry stream skimmed from a leased monitoring circuit and preserved, unread for centuries, in the **Wasatch Corpus**; the archipelago itself appears in the record only as traffic. Halvorsen, E. models the housing — titanium alloy, effectively immune to seawater corrosion at depth — as structurally intact, and it was an unrelated seabed survey, imaging the builder's plate long after the fault, that returned the unit its name. Ibarra, N. cites it as the literal case of single-filament infrastructure: one filament, on the seabed, singular by design.

## Notes

No repair ship's log survives to say whether one was ever dispatched. Readings divide on the unfunded second spur: one school holds it was a known point of failure knowingly left unrepaired; another that declining to buy redundancy for a route whose traffic was already falling was, on the era's own arithmetic, a defensible decision. The record supports both and settles neither.
