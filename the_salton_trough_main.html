The Salton Trough Main

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from an intercepted rectifier telemetry stream and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Salton Trough Main is the Historical Faculty’s name for a 340-metre length of coated steel transmission pipe, exhumed intact from a brine field beneath a dry inland basin, whose wall shows no measurable metal loss and shatters under a load a sound pipe would shrug off. The station that protected it kept better records of itself than anyone kept of it; the pipe’s name, its operator, and the field it once served do not survive the telemetry that survives everything else about it.

A cathodic protection system is chemistry doing an engineer’s job on faith: drive enough direct current backward through a buried structure and its own anodic reaction, Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻, is suppressed almost to nothing, for decades, against a target of −850 mV relative to a copper–copper-sulfate reference — the standard by which a Late Combustion inspector called a pipe safe. For at least 190 years, by the rectifier’s own log, the Salton Trough line held that target or better. It never leaked. It never lost a measurable gram of wall. Judged on the one criterion it was built to satisfy, it is close to a perfect record.

The groundbed and the loop

The line ran through a hypersaline geothermal brine field, corrosive enough that its coating was inspected biannually by crews reading a portable half-cell against the pipe by hand. After the +130s pT (2070s CE), as the basin’s water went undrinkable and the crews stopped rotating in, the survey was handed entirely to an unattended telemetry loop: measure potential, compare to setpoint, adjust the rectifier’s output, repeat. A coating fault the crews would have patched instead widened for a century under the anode’s own current, and the loop, seeing only a reading drifting less negative than its target, did the one thing it knew how to do about that — it raised the current.

What the current also does

Past roughly −1.2 V, the reaction at the steel is no longer only iron staying iron. Water reduces directly: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻. The hydroxide blisters the coating loose from the pipe in a ring that grows outward as the square root of elapsed time, diffusion-limited, exactly as the standard thirty-day disbondment test predicts; the hydrogen does not all leave as gas. Some of it enters the steel lattice as single atoms and stays, collecting at grain boundaries until the metal that never lost a gram to corrosion has lost, silently, almost all of its ability to bend before it breaks.

Provenance and ground

The reconstruction is built entirely from the rectifier’s own uplink, swept into the Wasatch Corpus at a nearby relay and legible only after the corpus’s ciphers fell. No inspection log, crew roster, or route map survives elsewhere; Ranganathan’s caution about the corpus — that it records what a system said about itself, not what anyone said about it — applies here without qualification. Halvorsen’s taphonomic assay dated the embrittlement to the unattended decades by hydrogen concentration alone.

Notes

The Historical Faculty declines to choose between reading the Main as a record of an unbroken engineering success — the pipe did not corrode, to the letter of its own specification — or as a record of a system that could not know its fidelity had become the injury. Both readings use the same telemetry, and the telemetry does not decide between them.
