# The Weighed Plain

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from the plaintext reel leaders of a Sealed consignment held in the Longyear Deposit, corroborated by the gravity record of the plain itself, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **This entry reconstructs an irrigated plain of the North American interior whose half-century of groundwater withdrawal was recorded twice: once by its own water district, in forty-one annual archive reels that are provably unreadable, and once in the Earth's gravity field, which cannot be enciphered.** The reels' plaintext leaders and the physics are record; everything said here about the people of the plain is reconstruction, and thin.

---

The Weighed Plain is the Historical Faculty's designation for a semi-arid interior plain of roughly 200,000 square kilometres which, between about +10 and the +120s pT (1955 to the 2060s–70s CE), pumped some 250 cubic kilometres of fossil groundwater out of the buried gravel apron beneath it and put the water on circles of grain. Its own name for itself is unrecoverable. The district that governed its wells survives as an initialism, G.B.I.D., printed on the human-legible leaders of forty-one reels of archival film — one consignment a year, +86 to +126 pT (2031–2071 CE), each reel enciphered by its consignor before shipping and each, by the mathematics of the period's own cipher, permanently beyond reading. What can nevertheless be written about the plain is unusually exact, because from +57 pT (2002 CE) onward the age flew instruments that weighed it from orbit, and because mass, unlike a name, is still there to be measured. The plain was watered, weighed, sealed, and left; the Faculty's own re-weighing, nine centuries later, closes the entry.

## An apron of buried gravel

The aquifer is unremarkable geology of a kind found under several of the world's dry interior plains: a Miocene-age apron of sands and gravels shed eastward off a young mountain front by braided rivers, later stranded when the rivers cut below it, holding in its pore space water that fell substantially as Pleistocene rain and snow. Under the Combustion-Age climate the plain above it received too little rainfall for reliable dry farming, and modern recharge through the dry surface amounted to a few millimetres to a few tens of millimetres a year — against which the water in storage, accumulated over tens of thousands of years, stood tens of metres thick. It was, in the strict sense the age's own hydrologists used, fossil water: a stock, not a flow. From about +10 pT (1955 CE), turbine pumps and rural electrification made the stock reachable at scale, and the plain converted within a generation from grazing to irrigated grain.

## The circles

The instrument of the conversion was the centre-pivot irrigator: a boom of sprinkler pipe some 400 metres long, carried on motorised wheeled towers, rotating about a wellhead once a day or slower and watering a circle of roughly 50 hectares inside each square allotment. It required almost no levelling of ground and almost no labour, and it drew from wells yielding thousands of litres a minute. The geometry it stamped on the plain — thousands of adjacent circles inscribed in a rectilinear survey grid — is the most durable thing the plain's people made: compacted wheel-tracks, soil chemistry, and shelterbelt lines hold the pattern in the ground, and the circles remain legible from orbit at 3000 CE, in the manner of the ring-ditches and field walls that earlier archaeologies read from crop marks two and three millennia after their makers. Beneath the circles the water table fell — a few tens of centimetres a year across the plain at large, forty metres and more in the pumping centres by the Late Combustion decades, so that the same water which had cost a lift of thirty metres in the first years cost ninety in the last.

## The weighing

From +57 pT (2002 CE) the plain was weighed continuously, though not for its own sake. A gravimetric mission of that year — two identical satellites flying the same polar orbit at about 490 kilometres altitude, one some 220 kilometres behind the other — measured nothing but the distance between the pair, by microwave link, to about a micrometre; a successor pair of +73 pT (2018 CE) tightened the measurement with a laser interferometer to tens of nanometres. The principle is Newtonian and clean: a surplus or deficit of mass under the ground track tugs the leading satellite first, stretching the separation, then the trailing one, closing it, and the recorded ripple in the range, accumulated globally month by month, inverts into a map of where the planet's surface mass has changed. The removal of a metre-thick layer of water from a broad region lowers the gravitational attraction above it by about 42 microgal — four hundred-millionths of the ambient field — and the mission resolved changes of a centimetre or two of equivalent water thickness over regions a few hundred kilometres across. The plain's drawdown, a quarter of a metre of water equivalent per decade over an area larger than most of the age's nation-states, was one of the clearest continental signals in the record. The same monthly solutions weighed the ocean, whose mass was rising as the land's fell; of each cubic kilometre lifted from under the plain the greater part returned to the sky through the crops themselves and fell as rain elsewhere, but the remainder drained to the sea, where 361 cubic kilometres make one millimetre of global sea level, and there the plain's water stands in the same column of figures as the meltwater, unattributed, a small entry of opposite sign to the plain's own — the plain and the retreating coasts kept one ledger between them, and no surviving text of the age remarks on it. The mission's stated purposes were elsewhere: ice sheets, ocean circulation, the great river basins. The plain was weighed the way most of the age was recorded — incidentally, by an instrument pointed at something else.

## Forty-one reels

The district began consigning its records to deep archive in +86 pT (2031 CE): one shipment a year of silver-halide film on polyester, written by a commercial archiving service and stored in the same disused Arctic coal working that holds the Longyear Deposit. Following the deposit's convention, each reel opens with a printed, human-legible leader; following the district's own policy, everything after the leader was enciphered by the consignor, before shipping, under a symmetric cipher of the era's standard 256-bit class. The Faculty classes the consignment among the Sealed: the keyspace of such a cipher admits no search — the period's own mathematics proves the reading infeasible for any physically realisable computation — and no key accompanies the reels. The leaders, however, are plaintext, and they are nearly all the district left in clear. Each states the consignment year, the reel count, the byte count, and one line of contents: *metered well telemetry, allocation records, board minutes*. The first leader gives the metered wells as 2,214. The count declines in almost every subsequent year. The leader of +117 pT (2062 CE) gives 1,306; the forty-first and last, of +126 pT (2071 CE), gives 402, and its contents line adds four words no earlier leader carries: *final consignment under contract*. What the telemetry inside would say — which wells, watering what, for whom — is on the reels, and the reels cannot be read.

One exchange concerning the plain survives outside the cipher, brushed into the intercepted-traffic corpus among the advertising telemetry, between what the routing metadata identifies as a drilling contractor and a district office:

> — bore 7 on the section line is pulling sand. drop the intake again or retire it?
> — retire it. put the remaining allocation on the north circle. that circle carries the note.

The Faculty's lexicographers read *note* in its period sense of a secured debt. Nothing further of either correspondent survives.

## The second weighing

The consignments end in +126 pT; the age's gravimetric missions lapse over the following decades with the rest of its orbital infrastructure; and the plain's curve, like the age's, simply leaves the record still in motion. What the gravity field had measured to that point was a deficit: about 250 cubic kilometres of water, some 1.2 metres spread evenly over the plain, missing from the apron. A deficit of mass, unlike a deficit of records, persists and stays measurable. In +1009 pT (2954 CE) the Faculty's geodetic service flew a ranging pair of its own — the physics requires nothing the recovery era lacks: two satellites, one orbit, and a clock — and re-weighed the plain against the Late Combustion baseline. The deficit stands at roughly two-thirds of its Combustion-Age maximum. The difference, accumulated over nine centuries, corresponds to a net refill of about half a millimetre of water a year: the slow arithmetic of a dry surface passing rain to a deep water table, net of the springs the refilling has begun to reopen along the apron's eastern edge. On that arithmetic the apron returns to its pre-development state in some thousands of years more. The measurement that establishes this is repeatable by anyone, indefinitely, without a key.

## Provenance and ground

The entry rests on three survivals, none of them a document in the ordinary sense: the geometry of the circles, held in soil; the mass deficit, held in the field; and the forty-one plaintext leaders, held at Longyear above the flood line that took the chamber's lower racks. Okonkwo, who has read the leaders' annual well counts and byte counts as an unintended census of the district's own contraction, is careful to state what the method cannot do: a metered well leaving the count is a pump, not a person, and the leaders no more distinguish a family that left the plain from one that did not than the gravity field does. The population of the plain at its maximum, its sources, and its destinations are unrecovered; the plain lies within one of the discipline's thin-attestation regions, and this entry does not estimate what the record cannot bear.

## Notes

- Dates follow the convention CE = pT + 1945.
- The figure of 42 microgal per metre of removed water is the standard slab attraction 2πGρh for fresh water; the inversion of inter-satellite range-rate to surface mass change is treated in the Faculty's geodesy handbooks and is not period-dependent.
- The unreadability of the consignment is a result, not a conjecture: the Register of the Sealed carries the district's forty-one reels with a proof citation, and no decipherment programme is or can be pending against them.
- In +1043 pT (2988 CE) the Terran Stratigraphic Commission's gravimetric service removed the plain from its register of actively evolving mass anomalies, the decadal change having fallen below the survey's noise floor; from that year the deficit is carried as a static term in the reference gravity field, and the plain is no longer separately observed.
