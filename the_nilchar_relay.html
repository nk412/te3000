The Nilchar Relay

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from store-and-forward packet-radio telemetry retained by a regional carrier for its own network billing, recovered as a fragment of the Wasatch Corpus, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Nilchar Relay is the Historical Faculty’s name — taken from the sole surviving occurrence of the name, riveted to a dedication plate at the mast’s base — for a solar-powered packet-radio relay on a Meghna estuary char that carried the only outbound link for a cluster of maternity clinics running newborn haemoglobinopathy screening, until the channel did what a decade-old survey had said it would. The relay’s own engineering telemetry survives whole; the screening results it carried, and almost everyone who read them, do not.

The char the mast stood on had built itself from the Meghna’s own sediment sometime after +60 pT (2005 CE), the kind of low, mobile island the delta throws up and takes back on a cycle of years rather than centuries. A single VHF packet link ran from it to the mainland grid, chosen because the char’s population could not support a second one; every clinic downstream of it — four delivery wards serving a scatter of char and embankment villages — depended on that one path to file its returns. It is this single-path design, not any failure in the clinics themselves, that the record turns on.

What the channel carried

The clinics ran a heel-prick panel on every birth, screening dried blood spots by chromatography for structural haemoglobin variants. In this stretch of the Bengal delta the variant of consequence was haemoglobin E — a single substitution in the beta-globin chain, glutamic acid for lysine at residue 26 — carried, on regional surveys of the period, by well over a third of the local population, a frequency far above neutral drift and sustained only because the carrier state confers resistance to Plasmodium infection: classic heterozygote advantage, the same arithmetic that holds sickle-cell trait high across malarial Africa. The panel’s raw calls never crossed the relay; regional policy held results at the clinic and sent the mast only a nightly manifest — births screened, carriers flagged, samples pending — batched for the district laboratory. It is the manifest headers, not the genetics beneath them, that outlasted the age.

The survey nobody outran

A hydrological survey commissioned in +119 pT (2064 CE) modelled the channel’s migration under the char and gave the mast’s footing eleven years before the thalweg reached it. The channel reached it in ten. Rising wet-bulb summers upstream were, by then, already pulling the delta’s better-resourced clinics inland ahead of schedule, so the mainland side of the network thinned first, leaving the char’s single link to carry a growing share of the traffic before it carried none. A maintenance annotation from the mast’s last full year records, in the clipped tag-question register of the region’s own variety of the age’s vernacular: “footing gone soft again, replacement due, no?” No replacement came. The nightly manifests, and with them the char’s carrier counts, drop out of the aggregate; the mainland samples that kept reporting read, ever after, as the region’s whole story. The relay’s last packet crossed the mainland receiver at 03:14 on 14 September +129 pT (2074 CE).

Provenance and ground

The telemetry survives because the carrier serving the link logged every packet’s routing header for its own byte-metered billing, and that log was among the traffic skimmed at a nearby cable landing station and later read out of the Wasatch Corpus. Ibarra, N., whose standing subject is the single point of failure the Late Combustion economy built into so much of its infrastructure, cites the relay as a small, dated instance of the pattern: one canal, one ore body, two poultry bloodlines, one radio mast.

Notes

  1. Haemoglobin E results from a point mutation at codon 26 of the beta-globin gene (HBB); the substitution also creates an aberrant splice site, so the E allele is mildly thalassaemic as well as structurally variant.
  2. Carrier frequencies of this order are among the highest reported for any haemoglobin variant worldwide and are read, without dispute, as balancing selection against malaria; the exact regional figure the manifests imply cannot be recovered, only that it was high enough to be visible in a district laboratory’s monthly tallies.
  3. All dates are post-Trinity; CE = pT + 1945.
