The Coipasa Floors

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a carved drystone tally wall, a reused dedication lintel, and one unmarked grave on the eastern rim of the Coipasa salt basin, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Coipasa Floors are the Historical Faculty’s name for a set of Consolidation-era masonry frost-floors above the Coipasa salt basin, on which a high-plain settlement manufactured, by clear-sky radiation alone, the freezing nights that a warmed climate no longer delivered to its altitude. The walls, the eighty-eight carved winter lines, the lintel, and the grave are record; the settlement, the working of its trade, and the reading of its last winter are reconstruction.

In the winter of +244 pT (2189 CE), a mason on the eastern rim of the Coipasa salt basin cut the first of eighty-eight annual tally lines into the inner face of a drystone wind-wall standing at roughly 4,100 metres. The wall belonged to a stepped complex of shallow, stone-kerbed terraces — the Floors of this entry’s title — built some eight years earlier, at about +236 pT (2181 CE), by a settlement whose economy rested on a preservation practice far older than the Combustion Age itself: tubers spread out to freeze under the open winter sky, trodden to press out their thaw-water, and dried in the high sun, over and over, until they became a hard, near-weightless staple that kept for years. The practice required freezing nights. By the early Consolidation, at that altitude, the climate no longer reliably supplied them, and the Floors were the settlement’s answer: surfaces engineered to run colder than the air above them, so that frost could be made on nights when none fell. The complex worked for at least eighty-eight winters, kept its own accounts in stone, recorded its best seasons last, and stopped. What it was called, who worked it, and why the accounts end are questions the masonry answers only in part, and the parts do not agree.

The line that moved

Air cools with altitude at roughly 6.5 degrees per kilometre, so each degree of regional warming lifts any given temperature surface — including the surface below which a winter night freezes — on the order of a hundred and fifty metres upslope. The settlements of the high plain had sat, for many centuries of the deep past, comfortably above the line of dependable winter frost; the freeze-dried tuber was the form in which a harvest crossed from one year into the next, and the plain’s own dry-season sky — cloudless, thin, and cold — was the machine that made it. Several degrees of accumulated warming moved the line up through them. The practice could climb; the fields, the wells, and the peat seeps that watered the herds could not. A community that stayed where its water was found itself, by the +230s pT (2170s–2180s CE), keeping a crop it could no longer freeze at the altitude where it could still grow it.

The response preserved at Coipasa was not migration and not abandonment of the staple. It was construction: eleven terraced floors, each a shallow tray of fitted stone a hand’s depth deep, bedded on a thick layer of bunch-grass thatch, enclosed on the windward sides by drystone walls to about chest height, with the sky left entirely open above. Nothing about the design required the Combustion Age’s materials, and nothing recovered at the site includes them. It required only that its builders knew, or found again, a fact two other plains had exploited before them.

Winter by subtraction

Any surface open to a clear night sky radiates heat toward space faster than the sky radiates heat back down. Water, wet earth, and plant tissue are all near-ideal emitters in the thermal infrared — emissivity around 0.96 — and the atmosphere, if it is dry, is partly transparent to that radiation across the band from roughly eight to thirteen micrometres. The colder the effective sky, the larger the deficit: under a clear, dry column, a horizontal surface near freezing loses a net 40 to 80 watts per square metre, and the loss is greatest exactly where the Coipasa Floors stood — four kilometres up, above the densest and wettest part of the atmosphere, under the driest sky of the year. A surface losing heat at that rate, sheltered from wind and insulated from the warmth of the ground beneath, settles several degrees below the air. This is why frost forms on open ground on calm, clear nights when the air itself never reaches zero — a commonplace of the deep past’s own agricultural literature, where it ruined orchards — and it is the entire working principle of the Floors: the walls kept the wind from carrying warm air down onto the surface, the thatch kept the ground’s heat out from below, and the sky did the rest. Tubers laid a single layer deep on the stone froze through on nights the air stood three, four, even five degrees above freezing; shallow pans of water set among them grew a cake of ice by dawn.

The arithmetic of that ice fixes the scale of the operation. A ten-hour night at a net 60 watts per square metre removes about 2.2 megajoules from each square metre of floor; at water’s heat of fusion, 334 kilojoules per kilogramme, that is a ceiling of some six kilogrammes — a sheet of ice just over half a centimetre thick — per square metre per night, less whatever the night’s residual breeze put back. The recovered floors total roughly 900 square metres. The Floors were never an industry. They were a settlement’s winter, made by subtraction, one clear night at a time. Nor were they an invention without ancestors: the period’s own record preserves at least two earlier discoveries of the same trick on other plains — shallow straw-bedded pans that iced over under above-freezing air on the Gangetic lowlands, and desert ice-works of the same principle a continent further west, both of the deep past. The Faculty cannot say whether the Coipasa builders inherited the method from the surviving record or derived it again from watching their own ground frost. The physics required no archive.

The wall

The inner face of the highest wind-wall carries the complex’s accounts: eighty-eight horizontal lines, one per winter, each holding three numbers in the period’s decimal figures, punch-separated. A representative early sequence, in the Faculty’s transliteration:

· 114 ∶ 57 ∶ 11 ·
· 109 ∶ 63 ∶ 12 ·
· 118 ∶ 79 ∶ 9 ·

The first figure is read, without controversy, as nights tended; the second as nights that paid — nights the floors froze; the third, less securely, as loads carried down to the settlement’s store. Across the first seventy winters the pay-count runs a little over half the tended count, drifting with no visible trend. Across the last eighteen it climbs, and the wall’s four highest pay-counts are its last four. The two figures a modern reader most wants to set against each other — the count of nights that paid, and the fortune of the crop the Floors existed to preserve — are bound by a single quantity the builders had no instrument to read: the mass of water vapour standing in the air column overhead. Vapour closes the eight-to-thirteen-micron window from above, and vapour is also the only thing the plain’s rains can be made from; a dry column opens the sky to the Floors and withholds the wet season from the fields in the same season, by the same number. The wall’s best winters were its driest years. Okonkwo, D., whose method elsewhere reads commit timestamps and routing logs as unintended censuses, reads the Coipasa wall as an unintended rain gauge, inverted: eighty-eight years of high-plain drought history, kept in stone by people recording what they took the same figures to mean as fortune.

The eighty-eighth line, for the winter of +331 pT (2276 CE), reads · 101 ∶ 84 ∶ — the highest pay-count on the wall, and no third figure. The line is cleanly cut and simply unfinished. Nothing follows it.

Stone, and the dry sky

Halvorsen, E.’s taphonomic survey of the site (+997 pT, 2942 CE) is blunt about why this entry can exist: everything at Coipasa that was not stone is gone, and the stone survived because the same aridity that ran the Floors also preserved them. The thatch bedding, the trays’ fibre linings, the store, the dwellings, and every perishable record of the settlement have left nothing; the site attests a technique, not a town. The survey’s one find beyond the complex itself is a dressed lintel, recovered 700 kilometres north, reused face-inward in a later terrace wall, whose eroded dedication the Faculty can read only through a recovery-era gazetteer’s translation of a name then still current for the ruin: the floor that pays the sky. The vernacular name itself is not preserved in any form, and is not reconstructed. Inside the highest wind-wall, hard against the tally face, the survey also recorded a single grave, capped with two of the floors’ own stone trays. It is unmarked, and it is the only person of the settlement the record keeps.

Halvorsen’s wider point is the one the discipline now attaches to the site. A frost-floor built of stone in the driest habitable air on the continent is close to the only version of this technique that could reach the present; versions built lower, wetter, in earth and timber — and the survey argues, from the lintel’s journey alone, that there were others — would leave nothing at all. The Coipasa Floors may therefore be the sole survivor of a common Consolidation craft rather than one settlement’s singular answer, and the record cannot tell the difference: the archive of the technique was written by the same dry sky that ran it.

Two readings of the stopped wall stand, and the Faculty maintains both. One takes the reused lintel as the settlement itself, moved north with its craft when its water failed, the wall left unfinished because its keepers left before the loads were counted. The other takes the last line at its own valuation — the clearest, driest winter in eighty-eight years, on a plain where the clearest winters and the emptiest ones were the same winters — and reads the missing third figure as the count there was nothing left to carry. The stone records 101 nights tended, 84 nights paid, and no more; which of the two silences it is keeping, the stone cannot say.

Provenance and ground

The entry rests on the standing masonry of the eleven floors and their wind-walls; the eighty-eight-line tally inscription, legible in full; the reused dedication lintel and the gazetteer entry through which its meaning is read; and the unmarked grave. Halvorsen, E.’s survey of +997 pT (2942 CE) supplies the taphonomy; the reading of the tally’s second figure as a drought series follows Okonkwo, D. The radiative physics — the atmospheric window, the net nocturnal flux, the suppression of that flux by column water vapour, and the formation of frost under above-freezing air — is established and is independent of everything reconstructed here. The settlement’s site, its population, and its name are not recovered.
