The Neuquén Array

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from three autonomous ground-motion recorders recovered from the Neuquén Basin steppe and a satellite ground-segment operator’s bandwidth-billing archive, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Neuquén Array is three autonomous seismic recorders, physically intact but never once read, from a Late Combustion Age fluid-injection monitoring network on the Neuquén Basin’s northern flank. The recorders and their designation are record; the days of ground motion they were built to hold are reconstruction, drawn not from the recorders at all but from a bandwidth invoice.

Laser-etched into the polymer housing of each of three recovered units, and stencilled again on a transport crate found half-buried nearby, is the same designation: NEUQUÉN ARRAY. The three are autonomous ground-motion recorders — sealed cylinders the size of a fire extinguisher, each holding a three-component geophone, a GPS clock, and a flash memory card — recovered structurally intact from the arid Neuquén steppe, and none has ever been read. Their onboard compression scheme, proprietary to the field contractor that laid them and undocumented in any specification recovered with them, still holds whatever ground motion they logged; what is known instead of the array’s few days of measurable activity — when, how often, and roughly how large — comes entirely from outside the cards, from the plain byte-count column of a satellite operator’s invoices.

Three housings, one designation

Deployed in +88 pT (2033 CE) around a fluid-injection well on the basin’s northern flank, the array numbered at least fifteen nodes by their surviving GPS-timestamped configuration records, of which three — units 04, 11, and 15 — were recovered together in one unrelated mineral-prospecting sweep of the steppe, their polymer housings undamaged by anything but wind-scour after a millennium in a climate too dry to rot or rust them. Each carried a full charge of the era’s standard primary-cell battery, sized to run three months unattended; none shows discharge consistent with more than nine weeks of operation, which the Faculty reads as the length of the deployment rather than a failure of the cells.

What the bytes admit

Each node sampled its three channels at 250 times a second to 24-bit resolution — a raw rate near 2.25 kilobytes per second, or close to 194 megabytes a day per unit — then reduced that stream losslessly on board, predicting each new sample from the ones before it and coding only the residual, the era’s standard scheme for continuous waveform data. Once daily, each node surfaced a short burst to a passing relay satellite reporting battery state, GPS lock, and, for the ground-segment operator’s own capacity accounting, the exact byte size of that day’s compressed file — a field logged in plain, undecorated units for billing, immune to whatever proprietary encoding sealed the file itself. Quiescent ground gives a predictor almost nothing to correct and compresses hard, better than four to one against the raw rate; ground carrying real, complex motion defeats the same predictor and compresses far less. Across the sixty-one days the three units’ invoice lines cover, the byte count roughly doubles on eleven of them, and on one day very nearly matches the raw, uncompressed rate outright — a shape in a billing column that describes real seismic activity as precisely as a waveform would, without a single sample of that waveform ever having been decoded.

The invoices that should not have lasted

None of the array’s own memory cards contributed a byte to that reconstruction; everything above comes from the archived accounts of the Comahue Uplink Consortium, which its own charter obliged it to discard on a standard five-year commercial retention cycle. The disc set holding this client’s account survived that cycle by clerical accident: it had been cross-filed under an unrelated warranty dispute that stayed formally open for decades after the dispute itself was forgotten, which excluded it from every scheduled purge that took its correctly filed, and otherwise identical, siblings — and it survived physically because that particular batch of recordable optical media had been cut, by an ordinary supply substitution, from a phthalocyanine dye rather than the cheaper cyanine dye stocking the rest of the archive, a chemistry with markedly better resistance to the slow photobleaching that erased almost everything filed around it. The same discs carry the Consortium’s customer-account notes, machine-drafted dunning reminders standing beside human clerks’ handwritten exceptions in language so uniformly templated by +115 pT (2060 CE) that Belgrano-Achebe, R. could not, working from the text alone, say which entries any person had actually written. That an invoice built to settle a satellite bandwidth account, and nothing else, can be made to yield a seismic record no one meant to keep in it is treated in the literature on incidental record as one of the discipline’s cleaner demonstrations: the ground motion was never in the archive, but the arithmetic that recovers its shape was. No waveform the array recorded has ever been read; the shape of their sizes has.

Provenance and ground

The entry rests on the three recovered housings, cross-indexed by their etched unit numbers against the Consortium’s account records, and on the account records themselves — daily byte counts, GPS-lock timestamps, and battery telemetry, legible in plain commercial format and untouched by the recorders’ own undeciphered encoding. The compression mathematics — predictive residual coding and its dependence on signal complexity — is the period’s own established practice, independently verifiable and outside the reconstruction’s uncertainties; the injection well’s operator, the field contractor, and the content of every waveform are not attested in either fragment and are not reconstructed here.
