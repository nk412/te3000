# The Three-Tenths Setting

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a canal division's superseded record consignment — tracing-linen drawings, roster fair copies, and gunmetal gauge plates recovered from a worked-out gallery of a rock-salt mine — and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Three-Tenths Setting is the Historical Faculty's name for the self-acting water-apportionment works of an Early Combustion canal colony on a South Asian doab — a system that divided a snow-fed river among roughly a hundred thousand landholdings by masonry proportion alone, with no meter, no valve, and no keeper, according to a single crest rule cut into every outlet — and, by extension, for the rule itself.** The drawings, the rosters, and the castings recovered from the salt gallery are record; how the division ran in the field is reconstruction, because the registers that carried the days were written on a paper that destroyed itself.

---

The one personal name the works preserves is cut into the downstream coping of its head regulator, where the survey drawing of the finished masonry records it letter by letter: KARAM DIN, MISTRI — *mason* — beneath a year given twice, once in the civil calendar of the age, −53 pT (1892 CE), and once in the lunar reckoning of the mason's own community. **The Three-Tenths Setting was the apportionment machinery of a perennial irrigation canal driven across a dry upland between two Himalayan-fed rivers: a head regulator sanctioned for some eight and a half thousand cubic feet of water a second, four descending tiers of earthen channel, and, at every point where a smaller channel drew on a larger, a fixed masonry orifice proportioned so that its draught rose and fell in step with the channel it drew from.** The system watered somewhat over two million acres and the settler colony planted on them, and it did so without measuring anything: the division of the river was encoded once, in the geometry of several thousand outlets, and thereafter executed itself. Below the last outlet the same division continued on paper, in rosters that cut the week into timed turns at the field gate, one entry per holding, computed to the minute. Across five decades the rosters' entries multiplied — partible inheritance splitting each holding among heirs — while the quantity divided did not, because no refinement of the apportionment could add to what the winter river carried. When the basin's administration was partitioned at +2 pT (1947 CE) the division was reorganised out of existence and its superseded records were carted to dead storage in a spent salt working, where the drawings and rosters survived and the operating registers did not. What remains is a complete account of how the water was promised and almost no account of how it arrived, and the works' standing in the discipline turns on exactly that gap.

## The bar and the brought river

The interfluve the canal entered was called, in its own records, a bar: a high, well-drained tongue of alluvium between two rivers, carrying perhaps ten inches of rain a year — well below what dry farming needs — with sweet groundwater eighty feet down and more. It supported grazing camps and little else. The provincial scheme of −53 pT ran the main line down the bar's central watershed ridge, so that every channel below it commanded ground by gravity on both flanks, and settled the tract by allotment: rectangular grants of about twenty-five acres each, assigned to cultivators recruited from congested districts several hundred kilometres east. The colonists came by rail to the end of the line and walked the last stage to squares marked in bare ground. A sanction memorandum of −14 pT (1931 CE) quotes the tract's census at a little over a million persons; the census itself, like nearly all of the age's civil registration, does not survive, and the memorandum's one figure is among the few demographic facts about the colony that can be stated at all.

The canal's own name is not among what survives in its own script. The rosters' marginalia gloss it once, in the clerks' English, and the Faculty renders the gloss only in meaning: *the brought river* — or, in a minority reading that turns on a single retroflex character the hand does not close, *the guest river*. Either way the sense is the same fact stated twice: a river where no river was, arrived by arrangement.

Ibarra's school cites the works as a clean early type of *single-filament infrastructure*, the doctrine under which the age is read as running enormous dependencies through single points of failure: two million acres, a million people quoted in a memorandum, and every channel of four tiers drank through one masonry throat at the head regulator, closed by one set of gates, on one river.

## Division by geometry

The engineering problem the works answered is a problem of automatic allocation under fluctuation, and the province's canal circles solved it with what their own literature plainly understood as a control system built from masonry. The supply was never steady: the river rose and fell with the snowmelt, and the trunk channel's level rose and fell with it. An outlet that passed a fixed quantity regardless would take its full draught in shortage and pass the whole deficit downstream; an outlet cut as a plain gap in the bank would take more than its share in flood and invite enlargement by night besides. What the design wanted was an outlet whose draught was a constant *fraction* — one that took, at every state of the channel, the same share it had been sanctioned, so that abundance and shortage alike were distributed pro rata over every acre commanded, automatically, with nobody's hand on anything.

The rule that achieves this is arithmetic, and it is the period's own. An earthen channel run *in regime* — shaped so that its water neither drops its silt load nor cuts its bed, at a silt-stable velocity an engineer of an older canal on the same plain had published, from gaugings, as rising with roughly the two-thirds power of depth — passes a discharge that grows close to the five-thirds power of its depth. A free orifice passes a discharge that grows as the square root of the head standing over its crest. A small rise in the channel raises both, but at different proportional rates: the orifice's draught by half the rise reckoned against its head, the channel's by five-thirds of the rise reckoned against its depth. The two rates are equal — the outlet neither gains nor loses on its neighbours, but holds its fraction — exactly when the head over the crest is three-tenths of the depth of the channel. That ratio is the setting of the entry's title. It was cut, as a crest level, into every sanctioned outlet of the system: several thousand orifices, each proportioned once, each thereafter dividing correctly by shape alone. Where a channel was too shallow for a drowned crest the design used the weir-type alternative, an open flume whose draught rises as the three-halves power of head and which is proportional with its sill a tenth of the depth above the bed; the drawings show both, annotated with the fraction each was to take.

The province also possessed, and the drawings show it was offered, the opposite instrument: the rigid module, a device in which water rises through a spiral trough in free vortex and spends any excess head against a ladder of baffles, delivering a constant discharge to within a few parts in a hundred across its whole working range, indifferent to the levels on either side. The division declined it for general use, and the reason recorded in the sanction notes is an allocation argument, not a hydraulic one: a rigid outlet takes its full quota first and sends the entire shortage to whoever is furthest from the head. The proportional setting was chosen, in the design's own words as the drawings preserve them, so that "short supply shall be short for all." Who bears a deficit was decided once, in the choice of a casting, and never again by anyone.

What the system deliberately did not contain is as characteristic as what it did. There was no measurement in the loop. No gauge reading fed back to any gate; the daily registers existed to audit the works, not to operate them. The geometry was the controller, the set-point, and the actuator at once — and the design's political motive is stated without embarrassment in the sanction notes: a fixed masonry throat, its roof block sealed with a keyed course, cannot be argued with, bribed, or quietly widened without leaving broken stone as evidence. The predecessor practice of shuttered cuts tended by paid keepers had produced, in the notes' phrase, "adjustment"; the module produced arithmetic. It was, by the period's own account, the most incorruptible instrument of allocation the province operated — with the single vulnerability that a controller with no feedback cannot know when the plant has drifted out from under its calibration. Silt was the drift. A channel whose bed rises no longer stands in the depth its outlets were proportioned to, and a three-tenths setting in a silted reach walks quietly away from three-tenths; the remodelling programme that chased this — regrading beds, resetting crests — is attested in the drawings as revision upon revision, dated across four decades.

## The winter river

The supply the works divided was made by cold. The rivers of that mountain rim carry the year's precipitation twice: once as winter snowfall banked above the freeze line, and once as the summer melt that releases it, so that the river in flood ran many times — in the high weeks, tens of times — what it ran in January. The sanctioned full supply of the trunk was a summer figure. Through the cold-season sowing, when the colony's wheat wanted water most, the whole river at the rim fell below the sum of the sanctions drawn on it, and the division met the season the only way a proportional system can: the channels ran by published rotation, each tier taking its turns of days, the fraction cut but the fractions kept. A proportional outlet holds its share to within a few parts in a hundred through every state of its channel; nothing in the works added water. From October to March the river was what the cold had not yet released, and the sum of every share on every roster was that quantity and no other. The allocation was sized to the fact from the beginning: three and a half cubic feet a second, continuously, per thousand acres commanded — a deliberate thinness, spread protectively over the largest possible ground on the published reasoning that a famine is prevented by watering many fields a little rather than a few fields well. The works was an instrument for dividing scarcity evenly, designed by people who said so.

## The roster of turns

Below the last outlet the masonry's arithmetic continued as a document. Each outlet commanded a block of holdings sharing one watercourse, and each block's week — one hundred and sixty-eight hours — was divided among its entries in exact proportion to area held, in a roster fixing the day, hour, and minute at which each cultivator's turn began and ended. The rosters were revised as holdings changed hands or split, and fair copies of four revisions of one watercourse — three hundred and two acres on a mid-length distributary, its outlet's draught unchanged throughout at just over one cubic foot a second — survive on linen. The Faculty tabulates them as follows.

| Roster revision | Entries | Median holding (acres) | Median weekly turn | Smallest turn on the roster |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| −49 pT (1896 CE) | 11 | 26 | 14 h 28 min | 5 h 0 min (9 acres) |
| −33 pT (1912 CE) | 19 | 15 | 8 h 21 min | 2 h 14 min (4 acres) |
| −18 pT (1927 CE) | 31 | 8 | 4 h 27 min | 50 min (1½ acres) |
| −6 pT (1939 CE) | 46 | 5 | 2 h 47 min | 21 min (⅝ acre) |

The commanded area does not change across the four revisions, and neither does the water. What changes is the divisor. Partible inheritance split each grant among heirs generation by generation, every partition entering the roster as new entries with smaller areas and shorter turns, until by −6 pT the roster's last entry held five-eighths of an acre and twenty-one minutes of water a week. The division held exactly: the sum of the turns is one hundred and sixty-eight hours on every revision, to the minute.

Okonkwo's group reads rosters of this class as the age's unintended censuses, and this one shows both the method's reach and its floor. The quadrupling of entries in forty-three years is a demographic fact of some kind; it is not a count of people. An entry is a holding, not a household; a holding might feed one family or four; tenants, labourers, and every person without land are absent from the record entirely, and the census series that could convert entries to persons is lost with the rest of the age's registration. The one demographic series the works preserves runs in units no one alive can convert.

The rosters carry a second record their clerks did not know they were keeping. The marginalia are in the administrative English of the plain, and philologists cite them among the earliest working attestations of grammar the living tongue still keeps: the aspectual copula extended to stative verbs — one annotation reads, of a disputed turn, "the lessee is holding the land since two seasons" — and, entered against a crossed-out entry, the invariant interrogative tag whole. The water the rosters divided is nine centuries gone; the grammar their clerks were writing it down in is, by descent, the grammar of Standard Terran.

## The salt gallery

The consignment owes its survival to an administrative death. When the basin was partitioned at +2 pT (1947 CE) between two successor administrations, the division's records were split with the territory, and the superseded classes — completed drawings, closed rosters, filled registers — were carted for dead storage to a leased gallery of a worked-out rock-salt mine in the range at the doab's northern rim, a chamber already in use for the province's overflow paper. A roof fall, undated beyond its position in the mine's own working history, closed the gallery before the +60s pT, and it was opened in 2958 CE during a survey of the range's workings.

Halvorsen's taphonomy of the deposit is straightforward. The gallery was dark, thermally constant, and dry the whole year — the salt itself takes up what moisture enters, as the mineral does, and in air that dry mould does not run — and so the chamber preserved whatever its contents were made of. That is the whole of the bias. The drawings and roster fair copies were on tracing linen: rag cellulose, starch-sized, chemically at peace with itself, and after nine centuries still flexible enough to unroll. The daily registers — the gauge books, the running records of what each channel actually carried on each day of those fifty years — were on the period's machine-made wood-pulp paper, sized with an alum-rosin chemistry that leaves the sheet acidic, and the acid hydrolyses the cellulose it sits in. The registers required no flood and no fire; shelved in perfect conditions, they embrittled and fell to fragments by their own composition. The deposit thus preserves the works' every intention and almost none of its behaviour: the sanction survives and the days do not, a Svalbard bias in miniature, run on chemistry instead of shelf height.

One leaf is the exception. Pressed flat between two linen sheets, which buffered it, a single register page survives entire: a tail-gauge reading from the system's lowest distributary, undated beyond the season — a winter — recording the channel at rather less than half its sanctioned discharge.

## The two equities

Two readings of the works stand in the literature, and the deposit sustains both. The first takes the system at its geometry: the most equitable large allocation instrument of its age — a division fixed in stone precisely so that no officer, landlord, or crowd could move it, sharing shortage pro rata across two million acres by a rule anyone could inspect and no one could bribe, its rosters balancing to the minute across five decades. On this reading the shrinking turns of the table are the system working: the same water, honestly divided among more heirs, the thinness shared to the end. The second reading observes that proportionality is a property of a cross-section, not of a document. A three-tenths setting is proportional in the depth it was cut for; beds rise, reaches silt, and settings drift — the head of a silting channel draws rich while its tail draws poor — and the remodelling revisions in the drawings show the division chasing exactly this drift for forty years without ever stating that it had caught it. On this reading the linen preserves a fairness that existed at full supply on the day of sanction, and the fifty years of winters — the seasons when the division mattered — lived in the registers, which are dust. The tail-gauge leaf reads either way: a channel at half draught in winter is a rotation running as published, and it is a tail starving, and the leaf does not say which, and it is the only day of the works' fifty years that can be read at all.

## Provenance and ground

The consignment is held complete by the Institute for Deep Record Studies: one hundred and ninety tracing-linen sheets (survey and revision drawings, outlet schedules, sanction memoranda), the four roster fair copies of the tabulated watercourse with partial rosters of eleven others, three gunmetal gauge plates, two outlet roof-blocks, and the register fragments, of which one leaf is legible. Halvorsen supplies the taphonomy — the salt gallery's stable dryness, and the divergent chemistry of rag linen and acid wood-pulp — and Okonkwo's group the roster tabulations. The hydraulic doctrine in the entry is the period's own, restated: regime channels, the silt-stable velocity law, outlet flexibility, the three-tenths orifice setting and its nine-tenths flume counterpart, the rigid module and the grounds for declining it, and the week-of-turns computed to the minute. The division's name, the river's, and the colony's appear in the consignment only as initialisms of the superseding administration; the mason's name on the coping drawing is the deposit's sole personal name, and the canal's own name survives only in the glossed meaning given above.
