# The Electrolyte Line

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a struck nickel commissioning plate recovered from estuarine mud, read against a run of the plate's own telemetry in the Wasatch Corpus, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Electrolyte Line is the Historical Faculty's name for a daily structure-to-electrolyte potential reading, transmitted for forty-three years by the galvanic corrosion-protection system of a temperate-estuary wharf, that went on reporting the wharf's steel as protected after the wharf had stopped being one.** The plate and the intercepted readings are record; the water's height, and the fate of the steel above it, are reconstruction.

---

**Minus 1,020 millivolts, against a silver–silver-chloride reference, is the last potential the wharf at HOLME REACH reported for its own piles, in the summer of +171 pT (2116 CE), and by the only criterion the system was built to satisfy it is a good number.** The period held carbon steel in seawater protected at −800 mV or more negative; the reading was two hundred millivolts inside that line. The system was ordinary: fifteen tonnes of aluminium-alloy anode bolted to steel piles below low water, rated at 2,000 ampere-hours per kilogram — some 30 million ampere-hours, sized for thirty years — and a solar-powered unit that read a permanent reference cell daily and sent the figure to a maintenance contractor's server. It did precisely that. What it could not do is the subject of this entry.

## The line

Cathodic protection makes a structure the cathode of a cell and lets something cheaper corrode instead: an aluminium anode, at roughly −1,050 mV, drives current through the seawater into the steel and suppresses the steel's own dissolution. The current must arrive through the electrolyte. Steel the water does not touch receives none of it, however much anode hangs beneath, and the band just above high water — wetted, drained, oxygenated, salted, and dried on every tide — is where bare steel corrodes fastest: several tenths of a millimetre a year on published pile surveys, against a few hundredths fully immersed. The builders knew this and did the standard thing, sleeving the piles across the design splash zone and painting the deck steel above it. The line at which protection stopped was fixed by the water, and the design assumed the water would stay where the datum put it.

## What the water brought up

The period's own late assessments projected a likely global rise of one to two metres by +205 pT (2150 CE) on the trajectory it was following; over the system's life, on that curve, the estuary rose on the order of seven-tenths of a metre. Below the line, the rise immersed a fresh band of steel on every pile and the anodes took it up without instruction: warm water forms the calcareous scale that shields protected steel faster than cold, the current the piles drew fell rather than rose, and the newly wetted steel polarised within the season. The last vessel is recorded calling at +152 pT (2097 CE); the piles below the water were, on the reference cell's figures, better protected that year than in any year the wharf had worked. The sleeve did not move. By the +150s pT the splash band had climbed past its upper edge onto bare pile-head steel and the painted flanges of the deck beams — twelve millimetres of steel specified for the atmospheric zone, now losing metal from both faces at a rate that removes such a flange in under two decades.

## Forty-three summers

The unit's battery is inferred to have failed around +160 pT (2105 CE): thereafter the transmissions come only on bright days and stop each winter, a controller running straight off its panel. The readings do not change character.

At the mean current the run records, the anode store runs out near +173 pT (2118 CE). The intercept ends two summers short of that. Nothing in the last line distinguishes it from the first except the number, which stood nearer the anode's own potential than it ever had; what the cell read the following summer, if the panel lit the unit, went to a server the corpus does not carry.

## Provenance and ground

The plate was struck in nickel for the reason the period struck such plates in nickel, and was recovered by Halvorsen, E. from the anoxic mud of the reach, where the buried stubs of the piles it names still stand and everything above the mud line does not; the plate gives the wharf's name, the anode mass and rating, the design life, the protection criterion, and the monitoring unit's identifier. Okonkwo, D. matched that identifier to a telemetry stream in the Wasatch Corpus, skimmed from a cable landing station and read out with the rest of the corpus; the run is complete from +129 pT to +171 pT and carries nothing addressed to a person. The wharf's name survives on the plate alone.

## Notes

- Dating is post-Trinity: CE = pT + 1945. The telemetry run, +129–171 pT (2074–2116 CE), lies almost entirely within the Contraction (c. +133–195 pT / 2078–2140 CE).
- The −800 mV criterion, the ~−1,050 mV closed-circuit potential and 2,000 Ah/kg design capacity of aluminium-alloy anodes, the electrolyte requirement, the splash-zone and immersed-zone corrosion rates, the temperature dependence of calcareous scale, and the sea-level range are the period's own engineering and assessment figures, restated. The wharf, its readings, and the seven-tenths of a metre are the fragment's alone.
