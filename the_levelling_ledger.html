The Levelling Ledger

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a single bound fee book recovered dry from an upland strong-room and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Levelling Ledger is the Historical Faculty’s name for the surviving fee book of a recovery-era upland community whose trade was to certify, by comparing two optical clocks, how high a parcel of ground stood above the still-rising sea — the height read not as a distance but as the faster ticking of the clock carried up to it. The book, its transactions, and the physics of the measurement are record; the community’s own name, and how many of those it certified ever knew the datum was moving under them, are reconstruction.

Chronometric levelling is the determination of a place’s height above the sea from the rate of a clock, and the Levelling Ledger is the fullest surviving account of a people who lived by it. The community held station on the dry shoulder of an interior massif at 34.2° of latitude, 2,180 metres above the mean sea surface of its own day, in the second and third centuries of the Consolidation. Its instruments were two optical-lattice frequency standards — machinery of the burning centuries, brought by the age’s own laboratories to a fractional uncertainty near one part in ten to the eighteenth, and kept running, or rebuilt to run, long after the age that made them. One clock stayed fixed at a benchmark tied to the sea datum. The other was carried by hand to each parcel of ground a buyer wished to have weighed, and the two rates were beaten against one another over a glass line strung along the old road. The higher clock ran faster, in exact and calculable proportion to the height between them, and that difference — not a tape, not a level, not a sightline — was the height the community sold. The book records the fee, the parcel, the measured offset, and a stamp of approval, across twenty-eight years, in a bound volume that survived because the strong-room that held it stayed dry. Of the fifteen hundred parcels it raises above the sea, the sea has since reached more than two hundred.

The reference on the shoulder

The station’s place is fixed to the metre in the book’s own front matter, which reproduces the benchmark’s geodetic coordinates and the epoch at which its height above the sea was tied down: the datum of +300 pT (2245 CE), by which every later certification is reckoned. The choice of the shoulder was not scenic. It was the highest ground with a maintained road to the drowning lowland, and the community that settled it was one of the poleward-and-upland arrivals whose journeys the wider record does not keep — a people known to their neighbours by a name the book renders only in a clerk’s transliteration, its sense recovered as roughly those who weigh the up, its actual word lost with the tongue it was spoken in. They did not build the clocks. What they held was the older, harder skill: to carry a running optical standard up a mountain track without letting it stop, to hold it steady long enough to beat it against the reference, and to keep the road’s glass line unbroken so the two rates could be compared at all. The book’s earliest entries are corrections and re-carriages; the community was learning its own trade in its own ledger.

Weighing the height by light

A clock deeper in a gravitational field runs slower than one higher up, by the general-relativistic redshift of its light. Near the Earth’s surface the fractional rate difference between two clocks separated by a height Δh is

Δf / f = g · Δh / c²

with g the local gravity and c the speed of light — a shift of 1.09 × 10⁻¹⁶ for every metre of height, so that a clock certain to one part in ten to the eighteenth resolves a height difference of about nine millimetres. This is not an analogy for altitude; it is altitude, read directly. The quantity a clock actually returns is the difference in gravitational potential between the two sites — the geopotential — for the redshift is set by the potential well, not by geometric distance. And the geopotential is precisely the quantity that decides whether water comes to a place, because a body of standing water settles onto a surface of constant potential: the sea rests on an equipotential, the geoid, and “above the sea” means, exactly and only, higher in potential than that surface. A tape measure or a satellite fix gives a geometric height that need not say which way water will run. The beat of two clocks gives the geopotential, which does. The community was measuring, with light, the one number that governs the flood — and the age’s own metrology had proved the method sound, comparing transportable optical clocks across a kilometre of height and recovering the predicted 1.09 × 10⁻¹³ shift to within a few parts in a hundred.

What the book priced

The ledger is a transaction record before it is anything else, and read as one it is an unintended census of who could pay to know they were safe — the use to which Okonkwo’s method puts every accidental ledger, reading the fee column as a register of means. The parcels cluster where the road ran; the arrivals who bought a levelling first were those with standing at the road’s head, and the low, cheap, late-certified ground went to those who came last and with least, the same gradient the drowned coasts had sorted people by on the way up. Each approved parcel carries the clerks’ certifying mark, stamped in their own living speech and glossed here for its grammar rather than its content: yumi done wei-em, i hai inaf — an inclusive we, buyer and weigher bound in the one pronoun; a completed act marked by the preverbal particle descended from done; the interdentals gone, the whole closed by the invariant tag the tongue carried up out of the south. The book prices the height in the measured offset and its converted margin both:

The conversion is exact: each margin is the offset divided by 1.09 × 10⁻¹⁶ per metre, the height read straight off the frequency. What the table cannot show is the assumption folded into its last column but not its first — that the datum against which every margin is finite would stay where +300 pT had fixed it.

The line that would not hold still

It did not stay. The sea rests on an equipotential, and the equipotential itself rises as ice melts to water and the melt redistributes the planet’s mass; the surface every margin in the book was measured against was climbing while the book was being written. This was not discovered late. The rise had been given a rising curve in the age’s own science, published at +95 pT (2040 CE) and never once withdrawn, and the book’s own founding charter calls its datum, in the same clause that fixes it, provisional. The clerks weighed each parcel true to the centimetre against a line they were told in writing was moving. A margin of 0.9 metres certified at +467 pT was a true measurement of a real height above a real sea on the day it was struck, and a certificate of nothing by the time the child who inherited the parcel was grown. The community’s instruments never erred: a nine-millimetre resolution held against a datum that rose faster than that per year is a perfect ruler laid against a line being redrawn. Nothing in the surviving book suggests the weighers misread the potential. It suggests, flatly, that the potential they read was the wrong one to have promised — the present sea’s, when the promise was made against all the seas to come.

Two readings

The discipline does not settle what the community understood itself to be selling. One school takes the book at its charter’s word: a people who knew the datum was provisional, said so in their own founding clause, and sold an honest measurement of a present height to buyers who wanted exactly that and read the rest for themselves. On this reading the weighers are the age’s clearest-eyed heirs, pricing certainty by the centimetre and disclaiming it by the clause. The other, following the revisionist caution against reading intent into a surviving hand, holds that a charter’s fine print is not evidence of what a buyer at the road-head was told, or understood, or could have refused; that the fee column shows the low parcels sold hardest and last, to those least able to wait for higher ground; and that a true ruler sold against a false promise is still a false promise, whoever knew. The book fixes what was measured and what was paid. It does not fix what was believed, and the entries that might — the buyers’ own words — are not among what a fee book keeps. Across its twenty-eight years the Levelling Ledger certifies 1,472 parcels as standing above the sea. Two hundred and thirteen of those heights the sea now stands above.

Provenance and ground

The book survived by the same accident as its keepers: it was upland and it was dry. A bound paper volume in a stone strong-room on an arid interior shoulder is about the most durable configuration the recovery era’s ordinary records achieve, and the volume reached the Faculty nearly complete, its ink faded but its columns legible, its glass-line beat-notes and fee entries in one continuous hand for the first decade and several after. Halvorsen’s survey of the deposit attributes the loss of its final quires not to damp but to use — the last years’ pages are worn at the fore-edge, handled thin, consulted until they parted. Nothing about which entries survived tracks which parcels the sea has since taken.
