# The Unstructured Field

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from 214 intercepted credit-transfer messages in the Wasatch Corpus and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Unstructured Field is the Historical Faculty's designation for an unnamed Late Combustion Age remitter known only from the 140-character free-text line of 214 intercepted credit transfers, in which plain messages home gave way, over twelve years, to a private code that reached the field's information ceiling and stayed there.** The transfers, amounts, routing, and field text are record; the sender, the receiver, and what the last forty-one messages said are reconstruction, and the last of these is not available.

---

**The Unstructured Field is not a cipher**, and the Faculty does not shelve it with the Sealed. It is the free-text line of a cross-border payment, defined by the period's interbank message standard as *unstructured remittance information*, at most 140 characters, and the whole trace of one person who, from +97 pT (2042 CE) to +109 pT (2054 CE), sent money to one account and wrote in that line what would fit. The messages were skimmed at an Indian Ocean cable landing and read once the era's public-key cryptography fell. The sender's name is unrecoverable: the ordering-party field held it in the same restricted alphabet as the message line, which the name did not fit, and it survives in nine spellings that each discard something different. What a lossy map discards is not recoverable from what it keeps.

## The alphabet and its ceiling

The field's permitted character set was seventy-three symbols: the letters in two cases, ten digits, ten marks of punctuation, and the space. Seventy-three equiprobable symbols carry log₂ 73, or 6.19 bits, each; 140 of them carry 867 bits, a little over 108 bytes. The period's own founding measurements of its dominant language put printed text at about 4.0 bits per letter on single-letter frequencies, 2.3 bits over eight-letter statistics, and between 0.6 and 1.3 bits when a reader's whole knowledge of the language was brought to bear. A plain message therefore spent roughly a sixth of what the field could hold. The rest was redundancy: what let the message be read by someone who did not already know it.

## The drift

The first 106 messages are plain. Okonkwo, D., reads them as an unintended calendar: one transfer in most months, in a narrow band of amounts, carrying school fees, a medicine, the day of a call. One is quoted with the standard's own tagging in place:

> `<RmtInf><Ustrd>FOR AMMA MEDICINE AND SCHOOL FEE 2 MONTHS WILL CALL SUNDAY</Ustrd></RmtInf>`

From +103 pT (2048 CE) the field changes. The amounts rise, the interval shortens, the beneficiary's institution identifier changes twice, each time to a bank further from the coast, and the text ceases to be text. Estimated by universal compression across the concatenated fields, its entropy climbs from between one and two bits per character to 6.0 in the final forty-one messages, within three per cent of the alphabet's maximum. Marchetti, L., compares the change to the commercial telegraph codes of the Early Combustion Age, sold against a per-word tariff, in which a five-letter group stood for a sentence; the fee here was per transfer, and the scheme permitted the field once. Two people with more to say agreed a book and moved the message toward the ceiling. No arrangement of the seventy-three symbols carries more through the field than 867 bits, and the last messages are at it. A string at the full entropy of its alphabet is, to every test the Faculty can apply, indistinguishable from one chosen at random; the theorem does not say that it is random, only that nobody without the book can tell.

## The return

The 214th transfer was not credited. The status report carries the external code set's reason `AC04`, *ClosedAccountNumber*, and the field text of the transfer it refused. Whether a 215th went by another channel, whether the code had already said where the account was going, or whether the last message was the plain one never sent, the corpus does not hold. Bekele-Sandoval, T., prints all 140 characters.

## Provenance and ground

The 214 messages and one status report are the anchor; amounts, dates, and identifiers are literal, and the entropy figures are compression estimates on short strings, hence upper bounds. Bekele-Sandoval, T., *Ceiling text: private codes in intercepted payment narrative*, Historiography 771, is the source paper.
