The Manzil Line

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from an intercepted nightly radio data stream held for six centuries among the Sealed, corroborated by three stamped steel base plates recovered along the northeast road of the Iranian plateau, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The Manzil Line is the Historical Faculty’s designation for a thirty-one-station store-and-forward radio corridor on the high road between the Zagros passes and the Kopet Dag piedmont, whose eleven intercepted years of nightly message traffic — 6,114,972 messages counted, 517 of them readable — spent six centuries catalogued among the provably undecipherable before an archivist matched forty-six stray headers to a compression library preserved in the Code Deposit. The station plates, the framing metadata, and the 517 messages are record; the corridor’s population, its operators, and the meaning of its traffic’s rise and fall are reconstruction, and are marked as such below.

The Manzil Line was a chain of thirty-one low-power radio relays strung, at intervals of roughly thirty-five kilometres, along eleven hundred kilometres of the old northeast road of the Iranian plateau, built in the years around +137 pT (2082 CE) to carry short personal messages between the plateau’s filling upland settlements and the message exchanges of the basins beyond the mountains. Each night it emptied its queue through a forty-minute long-haul radio slot; to fit more of the queue into the slot, its builders ran their text compressor in the one configuration the compressor’s own specification warned against, a setting that made the stream unreadable from any point except its own beginning. The intended receiver never noticed, because the intended receiver could ask for lost pieces again. The only party that could not was the distant signals collector that recorded eleven years of the Line’s traffic without being asked to, and it is the collector’s copy — gapped, unrepairable, and to every statistical test a string of noise — that survived. Catalogued on that evidence among the Sealed, the age’s provably undecipherable cipher archives, the stream sat unread from the hoard’s first systematic survey in +309 pT (2254 CE) until +947 pT (2892 CE), when forty-six sixteen-byte headers scattered through it were matched, content-addressed, to a public compression library held in the Code Deposit, and the corridor’s traffic moved in a single afternoon from the permanently unreadable to the merely almost entirely unread. What the reclassification returned was not the archive but its arithmetic: every message the Line ever sent can be counted, dated, and placed, and 517 of them can be read.

A road already staged

The road the Line follows had been staged for two thousand years before the Combustion Age and did not need resurveying. Caravan traffic on the plateau’s trunk routes had settled, over centuries, on a stopping interval of thirty to forty kilometres — the day’s travel of a loaded animal — and had built at that interval: walled courtyard stations with wells, cisterns, and standing masonry, many of them ruinous by the Late Combustion but few of them gone. The builders of the Line reoccupied them. The engineering reason survives in the geometry: with standard atmospheric refraction, the radio horizon of a twenty-metre mast on level ground lies about eighteen kilometres out, so two such masts see each other at a little under forty — and a line-of-sight relay chain therefore wants its stations at almost exactly the spacing the animals had already chosen. The Faculty’s comparativists note that the age’s own first electric telegraphs had run the process in the other direction, seeding towns at the spacing their repeaters required; the Line hung its repeaters on a spacing an older technology of the same road had built for it, and took the walls, the wells, and the foundations as it found them. The masts were lattice steel, guyed to the caravanserai walls; the power was photovoltaic, banked in salvaged batteries; the short hops between stations ran on a few watts each.

Who stood under the masts is thinner. The plateau, dry, high, and interior, was by the +130s pT (2075–2084 CE) absorbing people moving up out of the river lowlands and the gulf littoral to its south and west, where the wet-bulb excursions that had opened on the Indo-Gangetic plain in the +120s pT (2060s CE) were by then a seasonal fact of the low ground. The corridor’s stations reappear in the traffic as source addresses only — numbered, never named. The name the Faculty uses appears nowhere in the stream at all. It survives on three hot-dip galvanised base plates recovered in the road survey of +951 pT (2896 CE) from stations eleven, nineteen, and twenty-six, each stamped, before galvanising, in the period’s Latin capitals: MANZIL 11, MANZIL 19, MANZIL 26. In several languages of the road the word meant a day’s stage; in others, a destination, or a home. Which sense the builders intended is not recorded.

Forty minutes of night sky

The Line’s short hops moved traffic up and down the corridor all day, but the corridor only touched the wider world at night. Its head station worked a long-haul skywave circuit to a message exchange in the northern basins, and skywave at that range is a nocturnal physics: after sunset the ionosphere’s lowest, absorbing layer recombines within the hour, and frequencies that die in daylight begin to carry a thousand kilometres and more. The exchange ran a timetable, and the timetable gave the Line forty minutes.

What forty minutes is worth is fixed by a theorem the period had held for a hundred and thirty years: the capacity of a channel is its bandwidth times the base-two logarithm of one plus its ratio of signal to noise, and a three-kilohertz allocation, at the signal-to-noise a long night path actually grants, puts the ceiling in the low thousands of bits per second. The Line’s modem — the period’s standard serial-tone practice, its reference implementation present and content-verified in the Code Deposit — ran a nominal 2,400 bits per second and fell back by halves to a floor of 75 as the path worsened. Across the eleven intercepted years, the effective rate, computed from the cleartext framing timestamps, averaged just over 200 bits per second once retransmission is counted: some sixty thousand bytes a night. Against that budget stood the queue. The corridor averaged sixteen hundred messages a night across the intercept and touched twenty-nine hundred at its peak; a message averaged a hundred and twenty characters; and a night when the sky was poor was a night the queue held over, so that a family’s message could stand three, five, nine nights in a station’s memory waiting for its share of the forty minutes. Every byte the Line could avoid sending was another message that went tonight instead of next week.

Interval zero

The bytes were found in the usual place. The Line’s software — identified in the Code Deposit by the forty-six headers, and recovered there complete — compressed the queue with an adaptive coder of the prediction-by-partial-matching family: it keeps running counts of which symbol has followed which contexts, predicts each next symbol from the counts, and feeds the prediction to an arithmetic coder that spends fewer bits the less surprising the symbol proves to be. Everything the model knows it has learned from the stream itself, which is the design’s whole economy — sender and receiver each build the identical model from the identical history, so the model never has to be transmitted — and its whole fragility, because the coder’s state at any instant is a function of every symbol since the stream began, and a receiver who missed the beginning holds the wrong model forever. On the corridor’s message text the coder spent a little over two bits per character against the eight of the period’s plain encoding: each forty-minute slot carried well over three nights of uncompressed queue.

The format’s authors understood the fragility precisely, and provided for it. A reset marker — sixteen bytes of header, the model cleared behind it — could be inserted at any chosen interval, giving the stream fresh entry points at the price of a model that must relearn the language after each one. The specification, preserved in the Code Deposit and quoted here from its fourth section, states the trade in its own words: “Streams written with reset interval 0 achieve the best ratio and cannot be entered at any point other than their beginning. Loss is total for the remainder. Not recommended where any copy may be partial.” Every station on the Line ran at interval zero for eleven years, the stream’s state carried in memory from night to night so that not even a new evening began a new stream, and the setting appears in no recovered amendment, complaint, or note. Re-measured by the Faculty against the legible corpus, the saving over quarter-hourly resets was about four per cent — on an average night, room for some sixty further messages. The corridor’s own copy was never partial; the exchange, when a fade cost it a block, asked, and the protocol resent until the checksum passed. The one copy that was partial is the one that survived.

Filed with the ciphers

The collector that made the surviving copy was a bulk radio-frequency intercept station of the same custody, and eventually the same hoard, as the Wasatch Corpus — an instrument executing the period’s harvest-now-decrypt-later doctrine against everything its antennas could hear, the Line’s forty minutes included. An intercept cannot ask. Where the exchange recovered every faded block by retransmission, the collector recorded what the sky delivered, dropouts included, and in a stream written at interval zero a single lost symbol ends not the block and not the night but every night after it, until the next time the head station’s model is cleared. The collector’s eleven years of the Line are therefore continuous cleartext framing wrapped around a payload that is, except in forty-six places, permanently out of register with any decoder that will ever exist.

It was catalogued accordingly. A coder that has removed everything predictable and a cipher that has hidden everything predictable deliver the same object to an outside reader — a string with no handle, no skewed frequencies, no repeated structure, nothing an analyst can grip — because prediction is the only purchase an outside reader has, and both processes exist, for opposite reasons, to leave none. This is the one physical fact that binds the two kinds of object, and for six centuries it filed the corridor’s family messages beside the age’s sealed state and financial archives as one undifferentiated class: known to exist, known to contain something, presumed enciphered, provably beyond reading. The reclassification of +947 pT (2892 CE) is credited to an archivist of the Institute for Deep Record Studies working under Ashworth-Nakamura, K., who ran the hoard’s unclassified residue against the Code Deposit’s format signatures and matched the forty-six headers — each one a cold start of the head station, a battery failure or a hardware change clearing the model and beginning, for a few minutes, a stream the outside world could enter. The discipline’s aphorism for the intercepted record, Ranganathan’s, is that we do not know what they said to each other, only what was said about them to machines. The Line is the aphorism’s precise inversion and its confirmation at once: eleven years of exactly what they said to each other, held byte for byte, and closed.

Cold starts

Each of the forty-six entry points opens a legible run that lasts until the collector’s copy first differs from the corridor’s by one symbol — on a long skywave path, minutes, occasionally most of an hour. The runs together yield 517 messages. The longest, following the cold start of +144 pT (2089 CE), carries what the model carried in its opening minutes:

0842116  M31>EXCH  svc  cold start after battery change. queue held 9 nights. 11,240 waiting
0842117  M31>EXCH  fam  arrived the upper town in autumn all four of us. ask for us now at the river exchange not the coast
0842118  M31>EXCH  fam  to the one who asks each month from the date grove. we are here. we are here. tell him
0842119  M31>EXCH  fam  is the school at the ninth stage taking children this winter. ours are seven and ten

The register holds across all forty-six windows: arrivals, standing monthly inquiries, wells, work, schools, and the repeated instruction to redirect a search from one exchange’s catchment to another. The Faculty does not gloss these and neither does this entry.

What the payload withholds, the framing gives. A link protocol must be readable before it can be decoded — a receiver has to find frame boundaries, addresses, and checksums in clear, and the exchange’s acknowledgement scheme required a running serial on every message — so the intercept’s unreadable years are nonetheless exactly countable, and Okonkwo, D., reading the framing in the discipline’s standing method for incidental metadata, has extracted from them the only census the corridor will ever have. The nightly count rises from the low hundreds at the intercept’s opening in +141 pT (2086 CE) to a sustained twenty-nine hundred by +147 pT (2092 CE), the originations spreading stage by stage up the corridor as stations came alive northeastward; it declines from +149 pT (2094 CE), evenly at first and then station by station, to under seven hundred a night by the intercept’s end in +152 pT (2097 CE). Zhou-Ferreira, A. reads the fall as the traffic of arrival completing itself — a message corridor for people in motion has less to carry once the motion settles, and the standing monthly inquiries thin in the framing counts exactly as answered inquiries should. The other reading notes that the corridor’s catchment was still filling when its traffic began to fall, and that the framing counts messages, not people. The intercept ends with the collector’s retasking, not the Line’s silence; no later fragment of the circuit has been found; the road survey of +951 pT (2896 CE) found the masts down at every station it reached and the plates at three. The highest serial the intercept carries, sent in the last recorded minute of +152 pT (2097 CE), is 6,114,972. The number of messages that can be read is 517.

Provenance and ground

The entry rests on the intercept hoard’s holding of the Line’s long-haul circuit — eleven years of nightly recordings, its framing continuous and its payload legible only from the forty-six cold starts — and on the three stamped base plates of the +951 pT road survey, whose galvanised steel Halvorsen, E.’s taphonomy credits with surviving the plateau’s dry air where every softer fitting of the stations did not. The compressor, its specification, and the serial-tone modem practice are present and content-verified in the Code Deposit; the reset-interval warning is quoted verbatim. The information-theoretic ground — the channel-capacity theorem, the prefix-dependence of adaptive arithmetic coding, the statistical indistinguishability of redundancy-free and enciphered streams, and the asymmetry between a receiver who can request retransmission and an interceptor who cannot — is the period’s own established mathematics and engineering, not this entry’s invention. The identification of the corridor’s users as displaced households is an inference from the 517 legible messages and the framing geography; their numbers, names, origins, and subsequent history are not in the record and are not reconstructed here.
