The Cat Column

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from the outfit-year returns of a boreal trading post, surviving as one reel of archival microfilm, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

A ledger from an unnamed fur post at a subarctic river confluence, whose two columns of pelts the Faculty read for three centuries as a census of a forest in which the predator outnumbered its prey. The columns are record; the forest they were taken to describe is reconstruction, and the reconstruction was wrong in a way the ledger itself explains.

Thirty-one cats to every rabbit is the ratio the post’s ledger returns for the outfit of −60 pT (1885 CE), its best year, and it was the founding figure of a reconstructed ecology. The post stood where a tributary met a northward-draining river above the fifty-fifth parallel; the ledger names it only by the two rivers and by the account book’s own title, Returns. It was worked from −105 pT (1840 CE) to −22 pT (1923 CE), eighty-three outfit years, and it booked roughly sixty-one thousand cat — the post’s word for lynx — against under nine thousand rabbit, the snowshoe hare, across eight cycles and part of a ninth. Read as a count of animals, the ledger describes a forest that no energy budget permits. Read as a count of what was worth carrying south, it describes the most-studied oscillation in population ecology, entered by hand by the people who were starving in its troughs.

The forest with too many cats

The first reading, made when the reel was decoded in the 2660s, treated the Returns as an unintended census in the sense Okonkwo would later formalise: an accidental record read for the population behind it. On that reading the ground carried, at peak, thirty-one lynx for each hare. A predator of ten kilograms that kills a hare of one and a half every day or two cannot stand in that proportion to its prey for a season, let alone a decade, and the discipline responded by hypothesising an unrecorded second prey — the third-column school, which held that the clerk had simply not booked the animal the cats were eating. The reading survives in the discipline’s vernacular as a Standard Terran tag, cat is more than rabbit, isn’t it, and in the summary tables, which still carry the ledger’s cat series as “lynx, count.”

What the column counted

The post’s own tariff, ruled inside the ledger’s cover, valued one prime cat at forty rabbit. A cat skin was carried; a rabbit skin, near-worthless in trade, was taken in only for the post’s own robes, dozens of skins to one, and the rabbit column therefore records the post’s need for blankets and nothing about the hare. The cat column is nearer the truth but not a count either, and here the ledger closes on itself: the ten-year cycle was established in −3 pT (1942 CE) by an ecologist reading two centuries of a chartered company’s returns of exactly this kind, and a return of this kind is the one instrument that cannot measure it, since a pelt total is animals multiplied by trapping effort, and at this post effort was set by the advances column, which the clerk extended each June against the previous outfit’s cat. The series is, in part, a record of itself. The Middle Combustion field censuses that replaced the ledger method found hares swinging more than a hundredfold and lynx from two or three to forty-five per hundred square kilometres, lagging the hare by one to two years — and the post’s column, read as effort-scaled, shows that lag, and nothing else.

The advances are flour. A hare is lean enough that a winter fed on nothing else fails for want of fat — the period’s own name for the condition was rabbit starvation — and in the years the rabbit column reads zero there was not even that. The ledger records the flour, the accounts closed in the June reckoning, and no cause. The fifth cycle’s low, −57 pT (1888 CE), closed nine.

Provenance and ground

The Returns survive as a single reel of silver-halide microfilm, part of a national archive’s Middle Combustion filming of a chartered company’s post books, consigned to the Longyear Deposit and shelved high: the Svalbard bias kept this post’s ledger and dissolved its neighbours’. The tariff inside the cover, and the clerk’s practice of advancing against the previous outfit’s cat, are on the same reel. The effort correction is Sørlie’s; the third-column school has no living proponent and one standing table.
