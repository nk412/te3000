The Quickwater Marks

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from the marginal glyph column of a single canal-toll ledger and a paired skeletal assemblage from a lock-side burial ground, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

The quickwater marks are the Historical Faculty’s name for a lost hand-notation kept by English canal boat crews between roughly 1800 and 1856 CE, used to flag hazardous flow conditions on the water ahead. The glyphs, their dates, and the toll entries beside them are record; the notation’s own vocabulary, its users’ own name for it, and the fate of the trade that kept it are reconstruction.

The quickwater marks are not a script. They carry no phonetic value, spell no word, and were never meant to be read by anyone outside a single itinerant trade. They are a closed set of incised or chalked glyphs — perhaps a dozen recurring forms, no more — cut into lock-gate beams and painted onto milestones along one summit-level canal in the English Midlands, each one a flag for a specific, physically real hazard lying somewhere ahead on the pound: a wave that had already passed and might return, a spring that should not be there. The crews who cut them left no grammar and no glossary, and what the trade itself called the notation does not survive; “quickwater” is the Faculty’s own coinage, drawn from the boatmen’s argot for turbulent water that appears nowhere in the ledger itself. What survives is a lock-keeper’s habit of copying whatever mark he found chalked on his own gate into the margin of his toll book — a habit the company tolerated for thirty-four years for a mundane reason: a hazard flag was also, on its own accounts, grounds for a boat to dispute a demurrage charge, and the keeper needed the note to settle the claim, not to preserve the mark.

The marks

Two hazards account for nearly everything the column records. The first is a wave. A loaded narrowboat under tow — some 21 metres long on a channel roughly 1.2 metres deep — moves well inside the safe range: shallow-water gravity waves travel at √(gh), close to 3.4 metres a second, and a horse at a walking tow holds the boat to under half of that, a depth Froude number a little over 0.5. But a snapped line, a bad sheer at a passing place, or two boats meeting badly at speed can, for a moment, drive the local disturbance close enough to that limit to shed a solitary wave — a single travelling hump of water that, unlike an ordinary wave train, does not disperse. It is a real and well-characterised object, its persistence a balance between the nonlinear steepening that would otherwise break it and the dispersion that would otherwise flatten it — laminar in the strict sense, not turbulent at all, which is exactly why it can cross a whole pound undiminished where an ordinary breaking wave would shed its energy into eddies within a few boat-lengths. A crew that raised one logged it, because a following boat needed to know a wave was loose on the pound long before it reached them, arriving without warning at a bridge hole with enough force to put a legging boy in the water. The second hazard is a boil — a small, localised upwelling at the toe of an embanked reach, the visible sign that water is finding a path through the fill rather than staying above it. Water’s kinematic viscosity is small enough, about a millionth of a square metre a second, that even a slow seepage exit is fully turbulent within centimetres of leaving the bank: a discharge of only a few centimetres a second through an opening the width of a hand already carries a Reynolds number in the thousands, well past the transition that separates smooth flow from chaotic, and the boil is visibly rougher than the water around it for exactly this reason. The underlying threshold is a hydraulic gradient very close to one, by Terzaghi’s later expression for the sand core of an ordinary embankment; past it, grains move with the water instead of holding it back, and the pipe that starts as a turbulent boil can widen for hours before anything above ground looks wrong. When the flight’s gates were rebuilt in 1861 CE (−84 pT), the retired timber was catalogued by a county antiquarian who read every incised mark on it, hazard-glyphs and genuine carpenters’ assembly numerals alike, as assembly numerals; the reading held in the local record for over a century.

The junctions

The crews who cut these marks were drawn almost entirely from one occupational population — families who lived aboard, worked a single network of pounds and locks for generations, and by most later accounts married within the trade far more than chance would predict. Endogamy of this kind, sustained across many generations in an itinerant, socially separate population, is genetically ordinary; what is notable is how it changes. Skeletal remains from a burial ground at a canal junction sixty kilometres from the flight show the boat-trade lineages’ endogamy coefficient falling by roughly half across the second half of the nineteenth century — not evenly, but in two steps that align with the years the canal lost its short-haul traffic to the parallel railway and its remaining boats were pushed onto longer, mixed hauls across networks that had not previously shared crews. The relaxation tracks the widening of the routes the trade was forced onto, not any recorded change in whom the boat families would consent to marry. Where the families who left the shortest hauls went afterward, the burial ground and the toll book both fall silent; whether the trade thinned because its people found other work or because it simply contracted with the traffic it followed is not a distinction either source can make.

The keeper’s column

The flight’s toll ledger runs from 1823 to 1856 CE (−122 to −89 pT) and carries a marginal glyph beside 2,286 of its entries — the keeper’s own transcription of whatever mark he had found chalked on the top gate that morning. Fifty-eight of those glyphs are the boil. In 1852 CE (−93 pT) the embankment above the fourth lock failed at a boil the ledger records nine days earlier; the pound above emptied in under ten minutes, and the one boat moored against the culvert did not clear it. The toll book’s own entry for the breach is a stoppage charge, waived, in the ordinary clerk’s hand. Fifty-seven of the fifty-eight boil-glyphs the column carries are followed within the week by a stoppage-and-repair charge entered in the same hand. One is not, and the toll book carries no fifty-ninth.

Provenance and ground

The ledger survives entire in a county record office, an ordinary paper archive with no exotic taphonomy behind it: kept dry, rebound once, and retained because the canal’s corporate successor held statutory toll rights into the following century. Okonkwo, D. reads the marginal column as an unintended register of hazard behaviour, kept faithfully by clerks who had no interest in the marks themselves and every interest in the disputes they settled. The genomic reading is drawn from an independently dated burial-ground assemblage at the junction, not from the ledger, and the two sources are combined by geography alone. Ibarra, N. cites the flight as a plain instance of single-filament infrastructure: one embankment, one pound, and nothing behind either.

Notes

The number of distinct glyph forms is given as “perhaps a dozen” because the column itself is not exhaustive; several marks appear only once and may be miscopies, individual variants, or genuine hazard classes the ledger happened not to need again. The notation’s own name, and the names of the crews who kept it, are not recovered.
