# The Horn's Word

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a living-language idiom of unreachability and one intact, silent electroacoustic siren head recovered on an interior upland, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Horn's Word is the Historical Faculty's gloss for a fixed phrase in the living language meaning "beyond the reach of warning," traced to a Late Combustion network of automated public-warning sirens whose guaranteed audible range was set, and then quietly unset, by the temperature of the air itself.** The idiom and one recovered siren head are record; that the two belong together, and everything the network was for, is reconstruction.

---

The Horn's Word is not a monument, not a message, and not a machine anyone thought to preserve. It is a turn of phrase — three morphemes in Standard Terran that mean, together, *past where the horn's word carries* — kept alive in ordinary speech long after the objects it describes fell silent. Behind the idiom stands a real installation: a distributed array of high-power electroacoustic sirens, sited across a hot interior basin to broadcast a single tone that meant *leave now*, and an automated controller that decided, storm by storm and hour by hour, which sirens to sound and which populations it thereby covered. The controller decided against a stored map of how far each horn could be heard. The map was built from measurements of an air that no longer existed by the time the warnings mattered most. Its own operating name is unrecoverable. What survives is one horn, mute; a hoard of intercepted test telemetry; and the sentence people still use for a person who cannot be reached in time.

## The horn and the tone

The recovered head is a folded exponential horn driven by a rotating chopper — a real and old design, unchanged in principle across the age: a fan forces air past ports that a spinning rotor opens and closes, and the interrupted flow radiates as a loud, low tone. The surviving unit sounds near 460 hertz. The choice was not aesthetic. Sound loses energy to the air as it travels, and that loss climbs steeply with frequency: at the low end a tone bleeds off at of order a decibel per kilometre, while a whistle an octave or two higher is gone — tens of decibels per kilometre — within the first valley. A warning that must cross a basin has to be pitched low, and this one was. The oldest heads in the network are Middle Combustion castings, some struck before +20 pT (1965 CE); the tone they made was the least negotiable thing about them.

Loudness set the rest of the budget. A point source spreads its power over an expanding sphere, so its level falls by six decibels for every doubling of distance before absorption is even counted. A horn loud enough to hurt at its mouth is merely audible some kilometres out and, past that, is a matter of whether the listener's ear can lift the tone clear of wind and the basin's own noise floor. On paper, and on a still, uniform day, the network's rings of audibility overlapped and the basin was covered.

## The shape of the air

Air is not uniform, and the tone did not travel straight. The speed of sound rises with temperature as the square root of absolute temperature — about 331 metres per second at freezing, near 343 at +20 °C — so wherever the air is warmer, sound goes faster, and a wavefront crossing a temperature gradient bends toward the cooler, slower side. This is the whole mechanism, and it is unforgiving. On a hot afternoon over dry ground the surface bakes and the air immediately above it is far warmer than the air a hundred metres up; the near-ground layer can steepen past the dry-adiabatic rate into a superadiabatic gradient. Sound then travels fastest at the listener's own height and slower with every metre of climb, so every ray launched even slightly upward curves higher still and keeps climbing. Beyond a certain distance no direct ray reaches the ground at all. That distance is the edge of an acoustic shadow — a region the sound simply does not enter, into which a horn ten times louder pushes its shadow boundary hardly at all, because the boundary is set by the curvature of the rays and not by the power at the source.

The reverse held at night. Under the nocturnal inversion the basin ran cold at the floor and warmer aloft; sound then bent back down, ducted along the ground, and carried far past its daytime range. The same horn covered two different basins depending only on the hour. The one it could not cross was the hot, still, cloudless afternoon — the exact condition of a heat emergency.

## What the controller knew

The network's controller was added late, in the +90s pT (2030s CE), and it did what such systems did: it executed a fixed model on live inputs and logged everything to a leased data circuit, from which the record was skimmed and, three thousand years on, read out of an intercepted telemetry hoard. Its coverage map was calibrated once, against a climatology drawn from the decades on either side of its installation. Through the +120s pT (2060s CE) the basin's afternoons grew hotter and drier and stiller; the surface gradient steepened; the daytime shadow crept inward until the guaranteed footprint of some horns had collapsed to a few kilometres. The controller did not know this. It sounded the network and reported the basin covered, against a map of an air that had drifted out from under it, while the grid it depended on grew intermittent — the same continental-scale failure catalogued under **The Wide Synchrony** — and cut the fan power on the hottest days from the other side.

The clearest evidence of the network's health is also the most misleading. Its self-tests fired on a fixed civic schedule, at 07:00 on the first of each month, and the surviving log is overwhelmingly these tests: line after identical line, *coverage nominal — test tone accepted at all sited receivers*. Seven in the morning, in that basin, still held the night's inversion; the tone ducted; every receiver heard it; the test passed. The discipline read that archive for two generations as the network's ordinary performance before anyone noted that it recorded one hour of one favourable regime and nothing else. The network was tested when it worked and used when it did not.

## The idiom and the tell

What outlived the hardware was the sentence. Comparative work across the basin's descendant dialects reconstructs a compound — a warning-source noun, a speech noun, and a boundary particle — whose literal sense is *the far side of the horn's word* and whose living sense is simply *unreachable, past help*. It is oral: it was never, so far as survives, written by the people who coined it, and it enters the record only because it persisted in the mouth. A recovered gloss line renders it plainly:

> *pas-t' horn-word — them-side, no-hear can.*
> "Past the horn's word — the other side, where none can be reached."

Around the recovered heads the genome carries a quieter fingerprint. The basin's people had held a tight endogamy structure for something like a hundred generations; across the hot decades it relaxes, and it relaxes in a gradient that follows the lines of the retreat — poleward and upland, out of the shrinking footprint — and not any change of custom the record can name. Whether that loosening was a widening of the world or the scattering of a people, the DNA does not say. It says only that they moved, and that the phrase they carried for the edge of safety moved with them and outlasted the horns, the controller, and the basin's own name. Per Ibarra's single-filament reading, the network was one horn and one map over each valley, and the map was the filament that broke. The controller's last surviving entry, timestamped to a hot mid-afternoon in the +150s pT (2100s CE), reads *coverage nominal*; it is carried in the record as the network's own claim and not as a finding, and is superseded.

## Provenance and ground

The anchor is linguistic. The idiom is attested across several descendant dialects of the basin's vernacular and reconstructs cleanly to a Late Combustion compound; its etymology, opaque to its own later speakers, is what let the Faculty connect the living phrase to a class of mute warning hardware. The single recovered siren head, found intact and toneless on an interior upland, supplies the acoustics; the controller's conduct is read from test-and-event telemetry skimmed at a leased data circuit and decrypted with the wider intercepted hoard. Coverage figures are the network's own claimed footprint, not a measured one — the model's map, preserved, of an atmosphere that had already changed. The basin's operating designation for the network does not survive.
