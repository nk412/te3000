The Tonnage Clause

Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from the commit history of a volunteer transcription repository swept into consignment L-III of the Longyear Deposit, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.

This entry reconstructs an unnamed guano-extraction concession on a rainless equatorial island, worked under indenture until the seabird colony it depended on failed to return from a single warm season. The concession’s own daybook did not survive the age; what follows is drawn from a public transcription of it, corrected folio by folio more than a century after its last entry, and preserved only because the correcting was done in software.

It was not a mine, though its contracts read like a mine’s and it left a mine’s kind of pit. It was a guano station — one of a class of extraction sites the Early Combustion Age worked on rainless islands wherever a fish-eating seabird colony had been depositing nitrogen-rich droppings, undisturbed and unwashed, for centuries. This one operated from −96 pT to −67 pT (1849 to 1878 CE) on a small equatorial island in the eastern Pacific, cutting a deposit the Faculty’s taphonomists estimate accreted at two to three centimetres a year under the island’s near-total absence of rainfall, to a worked face some thirty metres deep by the concession’s final season. It closed in a single bird year, not because the deposit ran out, but because the animals that made it did not come back, and a contract clause that had functioned correctly for twenty-nine years went on functioning, correctly, into a season it was never written for.

A rainless deposit

The geology is unremarkable and the biology is not. Three fish-eating seabird species — a cormorant, a booby, and a pelican, in the reconstructed genus assemblage typical of the eastern Pacific’s cold-water coast — nested on the island in numbers the concession’s own surveys, cited in its prospectus, put at upward of a million and a half birds in a productive year. Guano is agriculturally valuable only where it is never rained on; nitrogen and phosphorus wash out of a droppings layer within a few wet seasons anywhere else on Earth. The eastern Pacific’s cold, upwelling-fed current suppresses local rainfall almost to nothing, which is the entire reason the deposit could accumulate for centuries and the entire reason it was worth a contract. Before the Early Combustion Age’s industrial fixation of atmospheric nitrogen — a process not commercialised until 1913 CE, a change this entry’s own reconstructed subject predates by decades — a rainless island with a large seabird colony on it was, in Ibarra’s later phrase, a single filament: one of a short list of natural nitrogen sources on which a continent’s cereal yield could be made to depend, with no redundant supply behind it.

The tonnage clause

Labour was indentured, recruited on multi-year contracts whose completion was defined not by a calendar date but by a delivered weight: the concession’s surviving prospectus specifies a term-ending tonnage of ninety long tons of guano loaded to the chute per labourer, credited against a fixed daily task, health and weather permitting, of a little over two hundred kilograms. Passage home was a separate, later clause, triggered on certification of the tonnage figure by the loading foreman. For twenty-nine years, with the colony steady, an able labourer cleared his tonnage in five to seven years and sailed. The clause was not designed as a trap. It was designed, competently, around an assumption the drafters never stated because it did not need stating: that there would be guano to load.

Where the birds did not follow

The colony’s food base is the anchoveta, a small schooling fish sustained by cold, nutrient-rich water drawn to the surface along that coast by wind-driven upwelling. The upwelling depends on a shallow, sharply layered thermocline; the cold, fish-bearing water sits within tens of metres of the surface, well inside the diving reach of a pursuit-feeding cormorant, which can sustain dives beyond forty metres, and comfortably inside the shallower plunge-dives of a booby working from the air. An irregular warm-phase reversal of the same current — recurring on a period the age’s own oceanographers had already begun to measure at roughly two to seven years, and known to it by the Spanish name for the Christ child, for the season around which it customarily arrived — deepens that thermocline by anywhere from several tens to over a hundred metres. The cold water does not vanish; it sinks below where a diving bird’s lungs and blood chemistry can follow it, a hard limit set by oxygen storage and pressure tolerance, not by hunger or will. The fish go with the water. In the concession’s twenty-ninth season, by its own daybook, no birds were seen returning to the north face of the island after the second week of September:

22 August 1878. Wind fair. No birds off the north face since the ninth. Gang two short — Li and the boy Tan struck off the tally, passage not yet due them. Loading continues per the old figure.

Population censuses taken elsewhere on that coast, later in the age, record losses in excess of four-fifths of a nesting colony within a single breeding season under comparable warm-phase conditions; no census of this island’s colony was ever taken, and the entry states no figure for it that the record cannot bear. What the daybook does record is that loading went on — not against fresh guano, which had stopped accumulating the moment the birds stopped nesting, but against the standing face, at the old task rate, because the tonnage clause specified a rate and a face, not a bird count. A labourer’s passage home was contracted to trigger on delivered tonnage, not on the calendar. When the colony did not return, the clause did not misfire; it simply had, for men already close to their figure, less and less left to complete it, and continued, correctly, to withhold passage from men with nothing left of the old deposit to load.

Who sailed

The concession’s resident manager and its loading foreman held salaried appointments, not indentures; their passage was contracted by the season, irrespective of tonnage, and the daybook records both aboard the annual supply schooner within six weeks of the birds’ failure to return — before the season’s shortfall was final, while the gangs’ own passage, tied to a tonnage the colony had stopped producing, was not. Which of the men struck off the daybook’s later tallies eventually left on a subsequent relief vessel the record does not itemise by name, and which did not leave the island at all, is a distinction the surviving folios do not preserve: the same mark, struck off, passage not yet due, serves the daybook for both, and nothing in the transcription disambiguates it. It is the same gap, at the scale of some forty men, that the age leaves at the scale of a continent — not that no one recorded the difference between a departure and a death, but that whoever kept this particular tally had no clause that required him to.

Provenance and ground

No paper daybook, ledger, or personnel record of the concession survives; the age’s tropical humidity and the site’s later abandonment are sufficient taphonomic explanation without invoking loss by any other cause. What survives is a public transcription project, begun in the early 2010s CE on a large public software-hosting service, that photographed and folio-by-folio re-keyed a privately held facsimile of the daybook for local historical interest; each correction was recorded as a discrete, content-addressed commit. The project’s repository was swept, incidentally and along with tens of millions of others, into the whole-of-internet snapshot now held as the Code Deposit — consignment L-III of the Longyear Deposit — where its bit-exact preservation owes nothing to anyone’s intent to preserve a guano concession. Okonkwo reads the repository’s commit timestamps, contributed by at least four distinct correctors over eleven months, as an unintended census of the transcription’s own labour, layered above the census the transcribed text itself could never take of the labour it describes.
