# The Dry Hall

*Terran Encyclopaedia, 3000 CE edition — Recovered Narratives. Reconstructed from a hand-kept ledger of 4,118 paper leaves, one box of Late Combustion fanfold printout recovered with it, and the configuration tables of one inventory program in consignment L-III of the Longyear Deposit, and rendered into Reconstructed Late Combustion English by convention of the Historical Faculty.*

> **The Dry Hall is the Historical Faculty's name for a Consolidation-era metal reserve that operated for one hundred and sixteen years inside a dehumidified cask-handling building on a high closed basin, keeping its accounts on the building's own inherited inventory system and its principal in an object that system had flagged, in the age's own hand, as not to be issued.** The ledger, the hall, the stock that remains in it, and the object at the head of every page are record; what the people who kept the ledger understood the object to be, and which lines of the ledger they wrote themselves, are not recoverable, and the entry is built around why.

---

**Ninety-one thousand four hundred kilograms, entered on the first line of a ledger kept daily for one hundred and sixteen years and never once drawn against, is the founding deposit of the Dry Hall.** The hall is a steel-framed building of the Late Combustion Age on the granite margin of a closed basin at 2,860 metres, built at +94 pT (2039 CE) to receive, inspect, and transfer welded canisters of spent reactor fuel, and kept dry — by a desiccant-wheel dehumidifier under the control of a building-management and inventory program — for the sake of the canisters and the instruments that handled them. The age's record of the site ends at +134 pT (2079 CE) with one loaded transfer cask standing on the hall's rail and the concrete overpack it was bound for standing open on the pad outside. At +231 pT (2176 CE) a community whose name for the building survives only in its meaning began to use the hall as a reserve for salvaged Combustion-Age steel, entering every bar, plate, and bolt into the inventory program by mass and drawing on it by account. The program priced the stock from a table it had last updated in +133 pT (2078 CE), refused to issue the object at the head of the stock list because of a class flag set a century earlier, and held the hall's air at a humidity setpoint chosen for sea salt, while the dust that blew in from the basin's dried potash floor was not sea salt. The ledger records some 2,900 tonnes of steel entering the hall and some 2,600 leaving it, a corrosion probe reading its own element to nothing, one attempt on the principal, and the day the entries stop. Three readings of what the reserve was contend over the same pages, and the pages cannot choose between them, because the column that would decide it — the memo column — is written in a mixture of the program's sentences and the clerks', copied out in the same hand, and no method the Faculty has separates them.

## The hall on the granite

The basin is one of the high closed depressions north of the plateau's main mass, floored at 2,800–2,900 metres, ringed by ranges above 5,800, and hyper-arid: its evaporation exceeds its precipitation by a factor above a hundred, and its floor is a salt plain thirty to forty metres thick in which halite is interbedded with the potash minerals carnallite and bischofite, the latter being hydrated magnesium chloride. The Combustion Age worked that floor for potash for a century and a half, pumping brine from beneath the crust and evaporating it in ponds, so that by the Late Combustion Age the plain's surface was a deflating pan of magnesium- and calcium-chloride residues rather than an intact crust of halite. The hall stands forty-one kilometres east of the pan's edge, on a granite bench the age had surveyed for deep disposal and used, in the end, only for interim storage at the surface.

The building itself is unremarkable for its period: a portal-framed hall of hot-rolled sections, 68 metres by 31, clad in profiled steel over mineral wool, with a rail running through it from the receiving bay to a transfer cell and, beyond the north wall, a concrete pad carrying forty-one vertical overpacks. Forty of the overpacks are lidded and closed. The forty-first stands with its lid on the ground beside it. Its loaded mass is given in the age's own printout as 91,400 kilograms — a cylinder of carbon steel plate around a gamma shield of cast lead, with a water jacket outboard of both for neutron shielding and, inside, a welded canister of austenitic stainless steel, wall 12.7 millimetres, holding thirty-two assemblies of fuel discharged from a light-water reactor. The cask on the rail was a transfer cask, a shielded sleeve for moving a canister between a transport container and its overpack; the age kept such casks indoors. It was standing loaded on the rail when the age's record of the hall ends, and it is standing there now.

The hall's control program is the reason the entry can be written. It was an ordinary building-management package of the +80s pT (2020s CE), managing the dehumidifier, the door interlocks, the crane, a rack of corrosion probes, and a stock inventory of every item on the floor by mass, class, and status, and it survived because its firmware sat in read-only memory, its stock file sat on solid-state storage that the program rewrote every day it ran, and its power came from a photovoltaic field on the roof with no battery behind it. Without a battery the program halted each dusk and restarted each dawn, and on each restart it printed a stock report — every item on the floor, sorted by mass descending — before accepting any transaction. Its open-source ancestor is held complete in the Code Deposit, and the site's configuration, transcribed into the ledger's opening leaves by a clerk who copied everything the screen showed, matches that ancestor's defaults in all but two values:

```
[site]
class        = 2        ; inland, low chloride
rh_setpoint  = 0.50     ; ref NaCl DRH 0.75; margin
[hold]
classes      = RAD, PRESS, HAZ
override     = supervisor
[corrosion]
probe        = ER, span 500 um, daily
```

The comment on the second line is the age's assumption written down where its successors would read it. Sodium chloride, the salt of marine aerosol, takes up water from the air and dissolves into a brine film on a metal surface at a relative humidity of about 75 per cent; below that the salt stays a dry crystal and, on clean steel, corrosion is negligible. A setpoint of 50 per cent is a comfortable margin under that line. Magnesium chloride deliquesces at 33 per cent, and calcium chloride at about 30. The program's site-class table, recovered from the Deposit, carries no entry for a basin whose dust is bischofite.

## The house in which iron does not sweat

Who the people of +231 pT were is thin in the record, as it is everywhere on the high interiors. The ledger's accounts run to 386 names over its life, written in the Latin letters the terminal keyboard offered, in a vernacular the Faculty reads as a contact variety descended from the age's dominant tongue with a substrate it has not placed. **Okonkwo, D.** has treated the account list, its openings and closings, as the unintended census it is; the Faculty's estimate from it is a community of a few hundred households at the reserve's peak, drawing on the hall from settlements along the basin's eastern rim. Their name for the building, of which only the meaning survives, the Faculty renders as *the house in which iron does not sweat*. The name is a description of the hall's air, and on the ledger's own figures it was wrong for the whole of the hall's second century.

The ledger opens on the eleventh day of the fourth month of +231 pT with a copy of the stock report the program printed on restart. The first line reads, in the clerk's hand, in the program's format:

```
2176-04-11 06:02  STOCK REPORT  site 2  rh 0.47  sp 0.50  er 000055
0001  TRANSFER CASK LOADED          91400 kg   RAD   HOLD
0002  LIFTING YOKE 100T              2860 kg   -     OK
0003  IMPACT LIMITER UPPER           1940 kg   -     OK
```

The report runs to 214 items, the spare-parts stock of a cask-handling facility: lifting gear, gaskets, bolting, trunnions, a drum of lubricant, a rack of instruments. The clerks entered their own stock beneath it — the first deposit of salvaged steel, 1,140 kilograms of reinforcing bar from a settlement on the drowned lakeshore, appears as item 0215 on the eighteenth day — and from then on every dawn's stock report opens with item 0001, at 91,400 kilograms, class RAD, status HOLD, and every dawn the clerk on duty copied it. The word the ledger uses for the item, in the vernacular, the Faculty renders as *the principal*; it carries in the accounts the sense of a sum that is not spent. It was a shielded steel cylinder on a rail inside a dry building, ninety-one tonnes of the best-finished metal in the basin, and the program's own report would not release it. The cask is a loaded transfer cask of the age's standard type, its canister welded shut with fuel inside it, and the class flag is the age's radiological hold. The ledger has no column for what the clerks who sat within a few metres of it for a hundred and sixteen years received from it, and the Faculty does not supply one; the nuclide that dominated the field at its surface through the ledger's century has a half-life of thirty years, and no measurable trace of it remains.

## Issue and return

The reserve worked as a bank works. Steel came in by mass and was entered against an account; steel went out by mass and was debited; the program valued each transaction from its price table and posted the balance. The table's last update, printed in its own header, is +133 pT — a list of unit prices by grade and section for a market that had ceased to exist when the update was made — and the program applied it unchanged for a hundred and sixteen years. The community's unit of account, in consequence, was a currency whose name survives only as the three-letter code on the price table, at a price for a kilogram of reinforcing bar that a Late Combustion merchant had entered in +133 pT, and the ledger's balances are stated in it to the end. No one on the basin's rim had any use for the number except as the number the machine used, which was sufficient. **Marchetti, L.** has observed that the reserve is the only recovered case of a Late Combustion commodity price surviving in daily use for more than a century, and that it survived by never being consulted.

The program's rules were the reserve's rules, and the ledger shows the community learning them. An item of class RAD, PRESS, or HAZ could not be issued without a supervisor override, and the override was a password the community never had; the ledger records 211 refused transactions against item 0001 in the first year, all carrying the program's memo string, and then a long silence on the point. An issue that came back within the reconciliation window at a lower mass than it went out was posted as *variance* and debited; one that came back heavier was posted as *variance* and credited. The credits are the corrosion, and they are the reserve's most regular entry. Steel converting to iron oxyhydroxide gains about six-tenths of its own mass in oxygen and water for every part of iron consumed; a bar that has lost a few tens of micrometres of metal to rust that has stayed adherent in still, sheltered air weighs more on return than on issue, by a few kilograms in the tonne, and the ledger credited it. Accounts in the hall's second century show small positive variances on nearly every return. The community's word for the credit, where a clerk annotated it, the Faculty renders as *growth*.

The ledger is a hand copy of the screen because the hall's line printer had no ribbon by +231 pT and the community had no way of making one. The Faculty has, alongside the hand ledger, one box of the age's own fanfold printout — 1,860 sheets covering +94 to +134 pT, boxed in the hall and found dry — and it is against that printout that the clerks' copies of the program's strings are checked. They are exact to the character. The clerks copied what the screen said, and then they wrote what they had to say, in the same column, in the same hand.

## The thirty-three per cent line

The hall's air is the entry's spine, and the ledger's humidity column is the record of it. For the first thirty-eight years of the reserve the dehumidifier ran: a desiccant wheel of lithium-chloride-impregnated media, regenerated by an electric heater on the roof array's midday surplus, cycling the hall's air past its setpoint and back. The clerks rebuilt the wheel's drive twice and repacked its media once, with a salt the ledger calls only *the drying salt*, bought from the basin's own floor; the accounts for those repairs are in the ledger, as are the program's daily *setpoint met* lines, which run unbroken from +231 to +269 pT (2214 CE). The wheel's bearing failed in the third month of +269 pT and was not rebuilt. From that day the hall's air was the basin's air, ventilated through the leaks in the cladding, and the program's line changed to *setpoint not met*, and the clerks copied that line every morning for seventy-eight years.

The basin's air is dry by any ordinary standard: the ledger's readings put the hall's mean relative humidity at 31 per cent across the year, with summer afternoons under 15. It is not dry by the standard that mattered. The hall's floor, walls, and stock carried a film of the pan's dust — the Faculty's assay of the surviving stock gives a surface loading of chloride between 60 and 300 micrograms per square centimetre, of which about two-thirds is magnesium chloride — and magnesium chloride dissolves into brine at 33 per cent relative humidity at ordinary temperatures, holds that brine down to well under 20 per cent once formed, and forms it again on any night the air crosses the line. In a basin at 2,860 metres the air crosses the line most nights of the year, as the temperature falls and the water it holds does not. The ledger's own dawn readings — taken at restart, near the day's minimum temperature — stand above 33 per cent on 71 per cent of the days between +269 and +347 pT. The hall in which iron did not sweat held its stock, on most nights of its second century, under a film of magnesium-chloride brine.

The program measured what that did. Its corrosion probe was an electrical-resistance element — a thin loop of the same carbon steel as the hall's frame, whose resistance rises as its cross-section is eaten, read once a day and printed as cumulative metal loss in micrometres over a span of 500. The age's printout shows the element losing 16 micrometres in its first forty years, a rate of 0.4 micrometres a year, the lowest band of the age's own corrosivity scale and the figure a dehumidified hall on a dry plateau ought to give. The hand ledger's `er` column shows 55 micrometres at the reserve's opening — a loss of 39 over the ninety-seven years the hall stood unattended, which is the same rate; the wheel had run on the roof's daylight power through the whole interval with no one in the building — and 70 at the wheel's failure. Between +269 and +303 pT (2248 CE) the column rises by 430 micrometres, a rate of about 12.6 a year, thirty times the design figure and squarely in the scale's second band, the band the age assigned to an unpolluted rural site in a temperate climate. At +303 pT the span was used up; the probe read full scale, the program printed the full-scale figure, `er 000500`, and the clerks copied it, unchanged, every morning for forty-four years.

**Halvorsen, E.** has confirmed the probe from the stock. The Faculty's re-survey of the hall at +847 pT (2792 CE) recovered the age's own rack of weight-loss coupons — flat plates of the reference steel, hung for exactly this purpose — and the residual stock, and read the rust on both. The outer layers are lepidocrocite and goethite, the ordinary phases of iron rusting in air. Against the metal, and through the pits, the phase is akaganeite, the chloride-bearing oxyhydroxide that forms only where the brine on the surface is rich in chloride and mildly acid, and which in the age's own literature was the mineral signature of marine atmospheric corrosion. There is no sea within a thousand kilometres of the hall. The chloride is the pan's. Halvorsen's mass-loss figures from the coupons, corrected for the age's forty years, give a mean of 12.9 micrometres a year over the reserve's second century, and agree with the probe.

The stainless canister inside the cask took the same air. The Faculty's borescope of the cask's water jacket, drained and dry since before the ledger opened, and of the canister's outer surface through the jacket's fill port, found the canister's circumferential closure weld carrying branched transgranular cracks of the kind the age's own engineers had described, in several volumes, as the hazard of exactly this combination: a welded austenitic canister, a chloride-bearing dust rich in magnesium, and a surface that has cooled enough, as its fuel's decay heat falls, to let the dust deliquesce. The age had specified that combination in its licensing literature as the reason such casks were kept indoors and dry. Whether the weld was cracked through in the reserve's century, or in the six that followed, the cracks do not say. They are through now.

## The soft metal

The one attempt on the principal is entered at +312 pT (2257 CE), and its shape is the hinge of the entry's controversy. On the nineteenth day of the seventh month the clerk entered an issue against item 0001 — 412 kilograms, in a category the ledger calls *soft metal* — to an account the Faculty identifies as a smith's, and wrote beside it a word the Faculty renders as *by hand*. The program refused the posting, and the refusal is copied beneath:

```
2257-07-19  ISSUE   0001    412 kg   soft metal   BY HAND
            sys: issue refused: hold class RAD; override required
```

The soft metal was lead. The cask's outer shell carries a cut window, 31 by 42 centimetres, made with a hand tool and closed again with a plate riveted over it; behind the window the gamma shield, some 70 millimetres thick, has been cut out to its full depth and undercut beneath the shell to either side over a length the Faculty's borescope puts at about 1.2 metres, and the volume removed — a little over a thirtieth of a cubic metre — corresponds, at the density of lead, to the mass entered. Lead melts at 327 degrees, casts easily, and was the scarcest workable metal on the basin's rim, and a smith with a cutting tool and a reason took it. For thirteen months the ledger the program kept and the ledger the hand kept disagreed by 412 kilograms, and every morning's reconciliation line — the program's, copied — records the difference. On the second day of the eighth month of +313 pT (2258 CE) the ledger posts a return of 412 kilograms of soft metal against item 0001, variance nil, and the reconciliation line clears. The lead in the window now is not the lead that was taken out of it: Halvorsen's assay gives it 6 per cent antimony and a trace of tin, the composition of a working smith's hardened casting alloy, where the shield around it is the age's soft, near-pure metal. Something of the same mass was cast back into the hole, and the plate was riveted over it, and the program's count came right.

## Three readings of the hold

The controversy is over what the reserve was, and it is argued from the same 4,118 leaves. The Faculty maintains three readings and adjudicates none.

The *specie* reading, whose strongest statement is **Sørlie, M.**'s, holds that the Dry Hall was a bank and the ledger is a bank's book: metal held on deposit, drawn against by account, valued by a fixed table, with a reserve of capital at its head that was never spent because the point of a reserve is that it is not spent. On this reading the community read the hold flag as its own rule — a principal that guaranteed the accounts — and the soft-metal episode is a default and a recovery: a debtor drew on the capital, the bank's books would not balance, and the capital was made whole. Sørlie notes that the ledger's balances stayed within the stock on hand for a hundred and sixteen years, which is more than the age's own banks managed, and that the reserve closed with its principal intact.

The *quarantine* reading, **Qadir, F.**'s, holds that the community knew what item 0001 was, in the sense that mattered, and that the reserve was the institution that kept it dry. The evidence is the wheel: the community spent, on Qadir's tally of the repair accounts, more of its recorded credit on the dehumidifier than on any other single thing, for thirty-eight years, when nothing in a metal bank required it, and a phrase in the memo column, entered in the clerk's own words twelve times across the ledger, which the Faculty renders as *keep the principal dry*. On this reading the name of the building is a statement of purpose, the class flag was understood as a warning, the lead was returned because the people who kept the hall would not have it opened, and the reserve failed as a quarantine on the day the wheel's bearing failed and it did not fail as a bank at all.

The *default* reading, **Tamang, R.**'s, holds that none of the rules were anyone's. The hold on item 0001 is the default hold-class list of an inventory package, recoverable from the Deposit; the refusal string is that package's string; the reconciliation that would not clear is that package's arithmetic; the price table is a stale file; *keep the principal dry* is, on Tamang's showing, within a word of the package's default maintenance prompt for any item of class RAD when the humidity setpoint is not met — a prompt the program would have displayed, daily, from +269 pT onward, in the same column the clerks copied. On this reading the community did not decide to hold its principal, or to keep it dry, or to put the lead back; it obeyed a configuration file it could not edit, for a century, because the file was on the screen every morning and the screen was the reserve. Tamang does not hold that the community was mistaken. The reading is that the question of what the community understood does not arise from the record, because the record was written by a program that understood nothing and by clerks who copied it.

The readings cannot be separated on the leaves, and the reason is structural. The memo column carries two kinds of sentence: the program's, generated from its templates and copied by hand, and the clerks', composed in the vernacular and written in the same hand. When the age printed the program's strings the fixed-width spacing of the printer identified them; in the hand copy that feature is gone. The clerks wrote their own memos in Latin letters because the keyboard offered no others and, by the ledger's second generation, in a syntax that has plainly been shaped by a century of copying the program's — the same clipped predicate, the same semicolon, the same order of noun and reason. The Faculty's stylometric separation of the column, attempted three times, assigns *keep the principal dry* to the program on two runs and to a clerk on the third. There is one line on which the readings would turn — the note beside the return of the lead, which the Faculty renders as *put back; the count is right* — and it is grammatical in exactly the way the program's refusals are grammatical, and in exactly the way the clerks had learned to write.

## Closure

The reserve's last decades are a drawing down. From +321 pT (2266 CE) the ledger's net flow of steel is outward in every year, and the account list shortens: 141 accounts open at the start of that decade, 63 at the start of the next, 19 in +345 pT (2290 CE). No account records where it went, because an account records a balance, and the balances were settled in metal and closed. Whether the households on the basin's eastern rim moved, or ceased, or simply ceased to need a bank, is the distinction the ledger was not kept to make; the ledger shows only that they stopped coming for steel, and that the steel they had returned over the previous century, with its small credited growth of rust, went out for the last time in lots of a few hundred kilograms to the accounts that remained.

The last leaf is dated the second day of the third month of +347 pT (2292 CE). It carries the dawn stock report, copied as it had been copied every morning, with the humidity at 0.38 and the setpoint not met and the probe at full scale, and the first line of the report is the principal. There is no closing entry, no annotation, and no leaf for the following day. The stock report as the clerk left it reads:

```
2292-03-02 06:14  STOCK REPORT  site 2  rh 0.38  sp 0.50  er 000500
0001  TRANSFER CASK LOADED          91400 kg   RAD   HOLD
```

## Provenance and ground

The ledger — 4,118 leaves of laid paper in eleven bundles, hand-written in one column in a succession of hands, dry, and legible throughout — was recovered from a steel cabinet in the hall's transfer cell by the Institute for Deep Record Studies' re-survey of the basin at +847 pT (2792 CE), together with 1,860 sheets of the age's fanfold printout from the same cabinet. The hall, the pad, the forty-one overpacks, the transfer cask on its rail, the residual stock, the coupon rack, and the corrosion probe are physical record and were surveyed in the same season. The control program's ancestor, its default configuration, and its hold-class list and memo templates are bit-exact record from one repository in consignment L-III of the Longyear Deposit; the site's own configuration is known only from the clerks' copy of the screen and is checked against the age's printout, with which it agrees. The deliquescence humidities of the chloride salts, the corrosivity bands, the resistance-probe method, the rust phases and their meaning, and the cracking of welded austenitic steel under magnesium-chloride dust are the age's own established corrosion science, and the corrosion figures given here are the ledger's and Halvorsen's coupon assays, not this entry's. That the reserve worked as described is read from the ledger and is robust. What the community understood item 0001 to be, and which sentences in the memo column were composed by anyone, are not recoverable. The community's name for the hall, the name of its currency, and the names of its 386 account holders beyond the Latin-letter forms in which the terminal recorded them are not recoverable either.
